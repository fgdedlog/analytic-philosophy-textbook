Learning objectives

  • distinguish the object language from the metalanguage, and ⊢ from ⊨;
  • state the soundness and completeness theorems and explain what each means;
  • explain why propositional logic is decidable but first-order logic is not;
  • state the compactness and Löwenheim–Skolem theorems informally and say why they are surprising;
  • explain, at an intuitive level, what Gödel's incompleteness theorems say and what they do not say.

Logic about logic

So far we have used logical systems. Metalogic studies them: it asks what can and cannot be proved in a system, and how proof relates to truth. The formulas of propositional and predicate logic belong to the object language; we talk about them in the metalanguage — here, English enriched with symbols like ⊢ and ⊨. The results in this chapter are theorems about logic, proved by mathematical reasoning in the metalanguage. We give the statements and the ideas, not the proofs, which belong to a second course (see the further reading).

Recall the two notions of consequence:

  • Syntactic (proof-theoretic): Γ ⊢ A means there is a proof of A from premises in Γ, using the rules of our natural-deduction system. This is a matter of symbol manipulation: a computer can check it without knowing what the symbols mean.
  • Semantic (model-theoretic): Γ ⊨ A means every interpretation making all of Γ true makes A true. This is a matter of meaning and truth.

There is no obvious reason why these should coincide. The first great results of metalogic say that, for classical first-order logic, they do.

Soundness

Soundness theorem. If Γ ⊢ A then Γ ⊨ A. Everything provable is valid.

Soundness says our rules never lead from truth to falsehood. The proof is by induction on the length of proofs: one shows that each rule preserves the property “this line is true in every interpretation that makes true the premises and undischarged assumptions it depends on”. For most rules this is easy — if A ∧ B is true, so is A. The delicate cases are the rules with restrictions: the restrictions on ∀I and ∃E are precisely what is needed to make the proof go through. (You saw in Chapter 7 that dropping them lets us “prove” invalid arguments; soundness would then fail.)

Soundness gives us a useful technique: to show that something is not provable, show that it is not valid by giving a counter-model. Since every provable argument is valid, an invalid one cannot be provable — however hard you try.

Worked example: using soundness to show that no proof exists

Claim: there is no proof of ∃xFx ∧ ∃xGx ⊢ ∃x(Fx ∧ Gx). Rather than trying (and failing) to find one, give a counter-model: a domain of two objects, one F and the other G.

Interpretation
  • Domain: {1, 2}
  • Extension of F: {1}
  • Extension of G: {2}
In this interpretation
  • ∃xFx ∧ ∃xGx is true
  • ∃x(Fx ∧ Gx) is false

The premise is true and the conclusion false in this interpretation, so the argument is invalid (⊭). By soundness, anything provable is valid; so this argument is not provable, and no amount of ingenuity will produce a proof. The same reasoning explains why the restriction on ∃E matters: without it, a “proof” of this invalid argument would be possible (Chapter 7).

The full soundness proof does this for every rule, and additionally handles subproofs: for →I, for example, one shows that if B is true in every interpretation making the assumption A (and the other live assumptions) true, then A → B is true in every interpretation making the other live assumptions true.

Completeness

Completeness theorem. If Γ ⊨ A then Γ ⊢ A. Everything valid is provable.

Completeness says our rules are strong enough: no valid argument is out of reach. For propositional logic this was shown by Emil Post (1921) and others; for first-order logic it was proved by Kurt Gödel in his doctoral thesis (1929, published 1930). The usual modern proof, due to Leon Henkin (1949), works by contraposition. Suppose A is not provable from Γ. Then Γ together with ¬A is consistent in the proof-theoretic sense (no contradiction can be derived). Henkin showed how to use the syntax itself — the names and sentences — to build an interpretation in which every sentence of this consistent set is true. That interpretation makes Γ true and A false, so Γ does not entail A.

Together, soundness and completeness say Γ ⊢ A if and only if Γ ⊨ A. This is why it does not matter, for classical first-order logic, whether we think of validity in terms of proofs or in terms of models; it is also why truth trees, natural deduction and axiom systems all agree on which arguments are valid.

Quick check Completeness says:

Completeness runs from ⊨ to ⊢. Soundness is the converse. "Every sentence or its negation is provable" is a different property (sometimes called negation-completeness), which Gödel's incompleteness theorem shows fails for arithmetic.

Decidability

A property is decidable if there is a mechanical procedure — an algorithm — that, given any input, always halts after finitely many steps with the correct answer “yes” or “no”.

Propositional logic is decidable

Truth tables give a decision procedure for validity in propositional logic: an argument has finitely many sentence letters, so finitely many rows, and checking each row is mechanical. (The procedure may be slow — the number of rows doubles with each new letter — and whether there is a fundamentally faster method is related to the famous unsolved “P versus NP” problem in computer science. But slowness is not undecidability.)

First-order logic is undecidable

In 1936 Alonzo Church and Alan Turing independently proved that there is no algorithm that decides, for an arbitrary first-order argument, whether it is valid. First-order logic is undecidable.

To prove such a thing, one first needs a precise definition of “algorithm”. Church used the lambda calculus; Turing used his abstract machines (now called Turing machines): a tape, a read-write head, and a finite table of instructions. The two definitions turned out to be equivalent, and the Church–Turing thesis says that they capture exactly the intuitive notion of an effective procedure. (The thesis is not a theorem, since “intuitive notion” is not mathematically defined; but it is universally accepted.)

Turing then showed that the halting problem — deciding whether an arbitrary machine will eventually halt on a given input — is undecidable. The idea is a diagonal argument reminiscent of the liar paradox: if a machine H could decide halting, we could build a machine D that, given a description of any machine M, consults H about whether M halts when fed its own description, and then does the opposite. Asking whether D halts on its own description yields a contradiction. Finally, one can write, for any machine and input, a first-order sentence that is valid if and only if that machine halts. So a decision procedure for first-order validity would solve the halting problem — which is impossible.

Semi-decidability

First-order validity is, however, semi-decidable: there is a procedure that will eventually say “yes” for every valid argument, though it may run forever on invalid ones. (Generate all possible proofs in order of length and check each; by completeness, if the argument is valid, a proof will turn up.) The non-terminating truth trees of Chapter 7 illustrate the point. Some fragments of first-order logic — for instance, arguments using only one-place predicates (monadic logic) — are decidable.

Compactness

Compactness theorem. A set of first-order sentences has a model if every finite subset of it has a model. Equivalently: if Γ ⊨ A, then some finite subset of Γ already entails A.

The second form follows quickly from soundness and completeness: a proof is finite, so it uses only finitely many premises. Compactness has striking consequences. For example, no first-order sentence (or set of sentences) can say “the domain is finite”. Suppose some set Φ were true in exactly the finite models. Add to it, for each number n, the sentence “there are at least n objects” (expressible with identity, as in Chapter 6). Every finite subset of the enlarged set has a model — a big enough finite one — so by compactness the whole set has a model; but that model must be infinite, while making Φ true. Contradiction. Similarly, first-order arithmetic has “non-standard” models containing numbers larger than every standard natural number.

Compactness thus marks a limit of expressive power. Second-order logic (which quantifies over properties) can characterise finiteness and the natural numbers, but it pays a price: it has no sound and complete proof system.

The Löwenheim–Skolem theorem

Löwenheim–Skolem theorem (downward form). If a countable set of first-order sentences has an infinite model, it has a countable model — one whose domain can be matched one-to-one with the natural numbers.

This yields Skolem's paradox. Set theory can prove that there are uncountable sets — the real numbers, for instance (Cantor's theorem). Yet if first-order set theory has a model at all, it has a countable one. How can a countable model contain a set that the model says is uncountable? The resolution is that “uncountable” inside the model means “there is no function in the model pairing this set with the natural numbers”. A pairing may exist from outside the model without being one of the model's objects. The moral, which philosophers including Hilary Putnam have pressed, is that first-order theories cannot pin down the size of their intended domain — a theme connected to debates about reference and indeterminacy (Chapter 10). (There is also an upward form: a theory with an infinite model has models of every infinite size.)

Gödel's incompleteness theorems

Do not confuse the completeness of first-order logic with the incompleteness of arithmetic. Both are due to Gödel, and they are compatible.

A theory is a set of sentences closed under logical consequence, often generated from axioms. Consider a theory of arithmetic whose axioms can be listed by a mechanical procedure (“effectively axiomatised”) — such as Peano Arithmetic, a standard first-order theory of the natural numbers with addition and multiplication. Gödel (1931) proved:

First incompleteness theorem. Any consistent, effectively axiomatised theory that contains enough arithmetic is incomplete: there is a sentence in its language that it can neither prove nor disprove.

Second incompleteness theorem. Such a theory, if consistent, cannot prove its own consistency.

The idea

Gödel's insight was arithmetisation: assign a number to every symbol, formula and proof, so that claims about provability become claims about numbers — claims the theory itself can express. He then constructed a sentence G which, in effect, says “G is not provable in this theory”. (The construction uses a “diagonal” trick, a cousin of the liar paradox in Chapter 16; but where the liar says “I am false”, G says “I am unprovable”, which leads not to paradox but to incompleteness.)

  • If the theory proved G, it would prove a falsehood (since G says it is unprovable), so it would be unsound — and, with a little more work, inconsistent.
  • So, if the theory is consistent, it does not prove G. But then what G says is true. So G is a true but unprovable sentence of arithmetic.
  • (Showing that the theory does not prove ¬G either requires a slightly stronger assumption in Gödel's original proof; J. Barkley Rosser removed it in 1936.)

What the theorems do not show

Gödel's theorems are among the most misused results in intellectual history. They do not show that:

  • “there are truths that can never be known” in general — G is unprovable in a particular theory, and can be proved in a stronger one (which will have its own Gödel sentence);
  • logic or mathematics is inconsistent or unreliable;
  • first-order logic is incomplete — it is complete, as Gödel himself proved;
  • uncontroversially, that human minds are not machines. J. R. Lucas and Roger Penrose have argued that we can “see” the truth of Gödel sentences that a machine could not prove, but most logicians reject the argument, partly because we cannot in general know that the relevant theory is consistent.

The theorems did, however, end the hope (associated with David Hilbert's programme) of securing all of mathematics by a single complete, consistent formal system whose consistency could be proved by elementary means.

Summary

  • Soundness: if Γ ⊢ A then Γ ⊨ A. Completeness (Gödel 1929/30): if Γ ⊨ A then Γ ⊢ A. Together, proof and validity coincide for first-order logic.
  • Propositional validity is decidable (truth tables). First-order validity is undecidable (Church, Turing 1936) but semi-decidable.
  • Compactness: if every finite subset of a set has a model, so does the whole set; first-order logic cannot express “finite”.
  • Löwenheim–Skolem: a countable theory with an infinite model has a countable model (Skolem's paradox).
  • Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931): consistent, effectively axiomatised theories containing enough arithmetic are incomplete and cannot prove their own consistency.

Key terms

Metalogic
The study of logical systems themselves: their soundness, completeness, decidability and so on.
Soundness
A proof system is sound if everything provable is valid: if Γ ⊢ A then Γ ⊨ A.
Completeness
A proof system is complete if every valid argument can be proved in it: if Γ ⊨ A then Γ ⊢ A.
Decidable
A property is decidable if there is a mechanical procedure that always gives a correct yes-or-no answer in finitely many steps.
Undecidable
Not decidable: no mechanical procedure always settles the question correctly in finite time.
Compactness
A set of first-order sentences has a model if every finite subset of it has a model.
Löwenheim–Skolem theorem
If a countable set of first-order sentences has an infinite model, it has a countable model.
Incompleteness theorem
Gödel's result that any consistent, effectively axiomatised theory containing enough arithmetic leaves some arithmetical truths unprovable.

Exercises

Exercise 8.1

Explain the difference between ⊢ and ⊨. Which theorem says that ⊢ implies ⊨, and which the converse?

Show solution

Γ ⊢ A: A is derivable from Γ by the rules (a syntactic notion). Γ ⊨ A: every interpretation making Γ true makes A true (a semantic notion). Soundness: ⊢ implies ⊨. Completeness: ⊨ implies ⊢.

Exercise 8.2

A student claims to have proved ∀x∃yRxy ⊢ ∃y∀xRxy in our system. Without looking at the proof, how do you know it contains a mistake?

Show solution

The argument is invalid: there is a counter-model (Chapter 6). By soundness, no invalid argument is provable. So the “proof” must break a rule — typically the restriction on ∀I.

Exercise 8.3

“Since first-order logic is undecidable, there are valid arguments we can never prove.” Is this right?

Show solution

No. By completeness, every valid argument has a proof. Undecidability means there is no single mechanical procedure that, for every argument, correctly answers “valid” or “invalid” in finite time. A proof-search procedure will eventually find a proof of any valid argument, but on invalid arguments it may run forever, and we cannot in general tell in advance which case we are in.

Exercise 8.4

Why does completeness of first-order logic not contradict Gödel's first incompleteness theorem?

Show solution

They concern different things. Completeness says every sentence true in all interpretations is provable. The incompleteness theorem concerns a particular theory of arithmetic: the Gödel sentence G is true in the intended interpretation (the natural numbers), but not in every model of the theory — there are non-standard models in which it is false. So G is not a logical consequence of the axioms, and its unprovability is exactly what completeness predicts.

Exercise 8.5

Explain informally why truth tables cannot serve as a decision procedure for predicate logic.

Show solution

A truth table checks finitely many rows. In predicate logic the analogue of a row is an interpretation, and there are infinitely many interpretations, with domains of every size including infinite ones; some arguments are invalid only because of an infinite counter-model. No finite table can survey them. (Church and Turing showed that no other mechanical procedure can do the job either.)
