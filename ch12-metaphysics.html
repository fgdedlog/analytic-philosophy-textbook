Learning objectives

  • explain Quine's criterion of ontological commitment and use quantifiers to state what a theory says there is;
  • outline realism, nominalism and trope theory about universals;
  • use Leibniz's Law in formal proofs about identity, constitution and persistence;
  • compare memory and psychological-continuity theories of personal identity and Reid's objection;
  • state regularity and counterfactual theories of causation;
  • formalise the free-will dilemma and outline the consequence argument and Frankfurt cases;
  • distinguish the A-theory and B-theory of time and outline theories of possible worlds.

Metaphysics asks about the most general features of reality: what there is, what it is to be one thing rather than two, what persistence, causation, time and possibility consist in. Analytic metaphysics fell out of favour under logical positivism (which declared it meaningless) but revived from the 1950s onward, partly through Quine's work on ontology and Kripke's on modality. The tools of Part II are used constantly: quantifiers for existence, identity for counting and persistence, and modal logic for possibility.

What there is: ontological commitment

Quine's essay “On What There Is” (1948) begins: “A curious thing about the ontological problem is its simplicity. It can be put in three Anglo-Saxon monosyllables: ‘What is there?’ It can be answered, moreover, in a word — ‘Everything’”. The interesting question is what a given theory says there is. Quine's criterion of ontological commitment: “to be is to be the value of a variable”. Regiment the theory in first-order logic; it is committed to whatever the bound variables must range over for the theory to come out true.

Example: commitment via quantifiers

“Some dogs are older than some cats” becomes ∃x∃y(Dx ∧ Cy ∧ Oxy): commitment to dogs and cats. “Some species are endangered” becomes ∃x(Sx ∧ Ex), so taken at face value it commits us to species — abstract kinds — unless we can paraphrase it into talk only of individual animals. “Pegasus does not exist” need not commit us to Pegasus: using Russell's theory of descriptions (Chapter 10), it says ¬∃x Px, where Px: x pegasizes (Quine's own coinage).

Quine's method is paraphrase: we are committed only to what we cannot paraphrase away. A nominalist who denies abstract objects must paraphrase “there is a prime number between 4 and 6” — no easy task, which is why Quine reluctantly accepted sets as indispensable to science. Recent “metametaphysics” asks whether such existence questions are substantive at all (Rudolf Carnap's “Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology”, 1950, argued that they are either trivial within a framework or merely pragmatic choices of framework).

Universals

Two red things resemble each other: they are both red. Is there one thing — redness — that both have? This is the problem of universals, one of the oldest in philosophy.

  • Realism: there are universals — repeatable entities wholly present in each of their instances. Plato placed them outside space and time (the Forms); Aristotle and, in modern times, D. M. Armstrong (Universals, 1989) place them in their instances. Armstrong holds that only the properties science needs — “sparse” universals — exist, not one for every predicate.
  • Nominalism: there are only particulars. Versions include predicate nominalism (things are red because “red” applies to them), class nominalism (being red is belonging to the class of red things) and resemblance nominalism (red things resemble each other, primitively).
  • Trope theory: properties exist, but as particulars — this apple's redness and that tomato's redness are two distinct tropes, which resemble each other exactly. Exact resemblance replaces identity of universals.

A standard argument against class nominalism uses identity of classes. Suppose (unrealistically) that all and only creatures with hearts have kidneys. Then “has a heart” and “has a kidney” pick out the same class; so on class nominalism they are the same property. But surely they are different properties. (The example is Quine's.) Formally, class nominalism identifies properties with the same extension, and the counterexample shows that sharing an extension is not sufficient for being one property.

Quick check On trope theory, what is the redness of this apple?

Tropes are particularised properties; different red things have numerically distinct but exactly resembling red tropes.

Identity and constitution

Recall Leibniz's Law (Chapter 7): if a = b, then whatever is true of a is true of b. Its contrapositive is the main tool for arguing that two things are distinct: find a property one has and the other lacks.

Example: the statue and the clay

A sculptor makes a statue, Goliath, from a lump of clay, Lumpl (the names are from Allan Gibbard, 1975). Goliath and Lumpl occupy the same place and are made of the same matter. Are they identical? Lumpl can survive being squashed into a ball; Goliath cannot. Let Sx: x can survive being squashed; g: Goliath; l: Lumpl.

So, if the premises are true, there are two objects in one place: the statue and the clay that constitutes it. Many find this puzzling (two things made of exactly the same atoms?). Responses: accept constitution without identity (Lynne Rudder Baker, Mark Johnston); deny that modal predicates like “can survive squashing” express genuine properties (Lewis's counterpart theory lets them shift with how the object is described); or deny that statues exist at all (eliminativism, Peter van Inwagen's Material Beings, 1990).

Note the converse principle, the identity of indiscernibles (if a and b share all properties, a = b), is much more controversial. Max Black (“The Identity of Indiscernibles”, 1952) imagined a universe containing only two qualitatively identical iron spheres — distinct, yet indiscernible, unless “being identical to this sphere” counts as a property.

Persistence

Objects change over time: the poker is hot, then cold. But if the hot poker is identical to the cold poker, Leibniz's Law seems to say that one thing is both hot and cold — a contradiction. This is the problem of temporary intrinsics (Lewis, On the Plurality of Worlds, 1986). Theories of persistence:

  • Endurantism: objects are wholly present at each time they exist. To avoid contradiction, properties are relativised to times: the poker is hot-at-t₁ and cold-at-t₂.
  • Perdurantism (four-dimensionalism): objects are extended in time as well as space, with distinct temporal parts at different times. The poker has a hot part and a cold part, just as a road can be bumpy in one place and smooth in another. No single thing is both hot and cold simpliciter.

(For the most famous puzzle of persistence, the Ship of Theseus, see Chapter 16.)

Personal identity

What makes a person at one time the same person as a person at another time? This is the question of personal identity — numerical, not qualitative, identity (see Chapter 1). Candidate criteria: sameness of soul; sameness of body or brain; or psychological relations.

John Locke (Essay, 2nd edition 1694, II.xxvii) proposed a memory criterion: a person at a later time is the same person as someone earlier iff they can remember (from the inside) that earlier person's experiences. Thomas Reid (1785) objected with the brave officer. A boy (b) is flogged for stealing apples. As a young officer (o), he remembers the flogging. As an old general (g), he remembers capturing a standard as an officer, but has forgotten the flogging. On the memory criterion, o = b and g = o, but not g = b. Identity is transitive, so this is inconsistent:

The standard repair (Locke-inspired theorists from H. P. Grice to Derek Parfit) replaces direct memory with psychological continuity: overlapping chains of memories, intentions and character traits. The general is linked to the boy through the officer. Continuity, being the ancestral of a relation, is transitive.

But continuity faces the problem of fission. Suppose my brain's two hemispheres are transplanted into two bodies, and each resulting person, Lefty and Righty, is psychologically continuous with me. They cannot both be me, since they are not identical to each other (they lead different lives) and identity is transitive and symmetric. Neither has a better claim. Parfit (Reasons and Persons, 1984) concluded that identity is not what matters: what matters in survival is psychological continuity (Relation R), which I have with both — so fission is about as good as ordinary survival. Animalists (Eric Olson) reply that we are human animals, and persist just as long as the organism does.

Causation

Hume (Enquiry, §7) could find no observable “necessary connexion” between cause and effect, only constant conjunction: events of type C are regularly followed by events of type E. The regularity theory of causation holds that this is all causation is: c causes e iff c precedes e and all C-type events are followed by E-type events. Problems: (1) day regularly follows night, but night does not cause day — both are effects of a common cause; (2) singular causation seems possible without any regularity.

The counterfactual theory of causation (Lewis, “Causation”, 1973): e depends causally on c iff, had c not occurred, e would not have occurred; and causation is the transitive closure (chains) of causal dependence. Problems include pre-emption: Suzy and Billy both throw rocks at a bottle; Suzy's hits first and breaks it, but had she not thrown, Billy's would have broken it. Suzy's throw caused the breaking, yet the breaking does not counterfactually depend on it. Much subsequent work (including Lewis's own later “influence” account, 2000) tries to handle such cases.

Free will

Determinism is the thesis that the state of the world at any time, together with the laws of nature, fixes a unique future. If determinism is true, can we act freely? Incompatibilists say no; compatibilists say yes. Incompatibilists divide into hard determinists (determinism is true, so no free will) and libertarians (we have free will, so determinism is false).

The dilemma

A classic argument against free will (versions are found in Hobbes and Hume, and in many introductions) runs: either determinism is true or it is not. If it is, our actions are fixed by events before our birth, so we are not free. If it is not, then our actions are partly a matter of chance, and random events are not free either. Let d: determinism is true; f: we have free will.

The argument is valid (it uses the law of excluded middle via TND). Compatibilists reject the first premise; libertarians reject the second, arguing that indeterminism located in the right place — perhaps in an agent's own causal power (agent causation: Roderick Chisholm, Timothy O'Connor) or in deliberation (Robert Kane) — need not make actions merely random.

The consequence argument

Peter van Inwagen (An Essay on Free Will, 1983) gave the most influential argument for incompatibilism, the consequence argument: “If determinism is true, then our acts are the consequences of the laws of nature and events in the remote past. But it is not up to us what went on before we were born, and neither is it up to us what the laws of nature are. Therefore, the consequences of these things (including our present acts) are not up to us.” Van Inwagen formalised it with a modal operator N, where Np means “p, and no one has, or ever had, any choice about whether p”, and an inference rule (β): from Np and N(p → q), infer Nq. Compatibilists have challenged rule β; McKay and Johnson (1996) showed that, in one formulation, it implies a principle about combining choices (agglomeration) that has counterexamples.

Compatibilism and Frankfurt cases

Classical compatibilists (Hobbes, Hume) define freedom as acting according to one's own will, without external constraint: the prisoner is unfree, the person who walks out of an open door is free, even if their choice was determined. Modern compatibilists add structure: Harry Frankfurt (“Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person”, 1971) says a free agent's first-order desires are endorsed by higher-order desires.

Many incompatibilist arguments assume the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP): a person is morally responsible for an action only if they could have done otherwise. Frankfurt (“Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility”, 1969) offered a counterexample, now called a Frankfurt case. Black wants Jones to perform an action, and has secretly installed a device that will force Jones to do it if Jones shows any sign of deciding otherwise. As it happens, Jones decides to do it on his own, and the device never activates. Jones could not have done otherwise — yet he seems responsible, since he acted entirely on his own. If so, PAP is false. The literature on whether Frankfurt cases really work is vast.

P. F. Strawson (“Freedom and Resentment”, 1962) changed the subject in another way: moral responsibility is grounded in our “reactive attitudes” — resentment, gratitude, indignation — which are part of human life and neither need nor could be justified by a metaphysical theory such as determinism.

Time

J. M. E. McTaggart (“The Unreality of Time”, 1908) distinguished two ways of ordering events. The A-series orders them as past, present and future — properties that change (the 2012 Olympics were future, then present, now past). The B-series orders them by permanent relations of earlier than and later than. McTaggart argued that time requires the A-series, but that the A-series is contradictory (every event is past, present and future, and attempts to avoid this lead to a regress), so time is unreal. Few accept the conclusion, but his distinction frames the modern debate:

  • The A-theory of time: there is an objective, privileged present, and time genuinely passes. Presentism adds that only present things exist; the growing block theory says past and present things exist, but not future ones.
  • The B-theory of time: all times are equally real (eternalism); “now” is indexical, like “here”, and the passage of time is a feature of our experience, not of reality. Relativity theory, which seems to deny absolute simultaneity, is often cited in support.

Prior's tense logic (Chapter 9) was developed partly to express the A-theory, treating tenses as operators rather than relations between times.

Possibility and possible worlds

Modal logic (Chapter 9) explains “necessarily p” as “p in all (accessible) possible worlds”. But what are possible worlds?

  • Modal realism (Lewis, On the Plurality of Worlds, 1986): other possible worlds are concrete universes just as real as ours, spatiotemporally disconnected from it. “Actual” is indexical: it means “this world”. Each individual exists in only one world; “Humphrey might have won” is true because a counterpart of Humphrey (someone sufficiently similar) wins in another world. Lewis admitted the view meets with “incredulous stares”, but argued that its theoretical benefits are worth the ontological cost.
  • Actualism / ersatzism (Alvin Plantinga, Robert Stalnaker, Kripke): only the actual world exists; other “worlds” are abstract objects — maximal consistent sets of propositions, or ways things could have been. Kripke (Naming and Necessity) insisted that worlds are “stipulated, not discovered by powerful telescopes”: when we ask what would have happened if Nixon had lost, we are talking about Nixon himself.

Using possible worlds, we can say precisely what the free-will and personal-identity debates need: e.g. determinism is the claim that any two worlds that share the same laws and agree at one time agree at all times.

Summary

  • Quine: a theory's ontological commitments are what its bound variables must range over; paraphrase can remove apparent commitments.
  • Realists accept universals; nominalists accept only particulars; trope theorists accept particularised properties.
  • Leibniz's Law shows distinctness from a difference in properties, as in the statue and the clay.
  • Endurantists relativise properties to times; perdurantists posit temporal parts.
  • Locke's memory criterion violates the transitivity of identity (Reid); psychological continuity fixes this but faces fission, leading Parfit to say identity is not what matters.
  • Regularity and counterfactual theories of causation face common-cause and pre-emption problems respectively.
  • The free-will dilemma and the consequence argument support incompatibilism; compatibilists reply with accounts of freedom as self-governance and with Frankfurt cases.
  • A-theorists believe in an objective present; B-theorists treat all times as equally real. Possible worlds may be concrete (Lewis) or abstract (actualism).

Key terms

Metaphysics
The study of the most general features of reality: existence, identity, causation, time, modality.
Ontology
The study of what exists; also, the list of things a theory says exist.
Ontological commitment
What a theory says there is; on Quine's criterion, the things that must be values of its bound variables for the theory to be true.
Universals
Properties or relations that can be wholly present in many things at once, e.g. redness.
Nominalism
The view that there are no universals (or no abstract objects); only particulars exist.
Trope
A particular instance of a property (this redness, here), as opposed to a universal shared by many things.
Particular
An individual thing that can be in only one place at a time, e.g. this apple.
Leibniz's Law
The indiscernibility of identicals: if a = b then a and b have all the same properties.
Identity of indiscernibles
The principle that things sharing all properties are identical.
Persistence
How objects exist through time — by enduring (being wholly present at each time) or perduring (having temporal parts).
Endurantism
The view that objects persist by being wholly present at each time they exist.
Perdurantism
The view that objects persist by having different temporal parts at different times.
Personal identity
The question of what makes a person at one time the same person as a person at another time.
Psychological continuity
Overlapping chains of psychological connections (memories, intentions, character) over time; on Locke-inspired views, the criterion of personal identity.
Regularity theory of causation
Hume-inspired view that causation is a matter of constant conjunction between types of events.
Counterfactual theory of causation
Lewis's view that c causes e roughly when, had c not occurred, e would not have occurred.
Determinism
The thesis that the past together with the laws of nature fixes a unique future.
Compatibilism
The view that free will is compatible with determinism.
Incompatibilism
The view that free will is incompatible with determinism.
Hard determinism
The view that determinism is true and incompatible with free will, so we lack free will.
Libertarianism (free will)
The view that we have free will and that free will is incompatible with determinism, so determinism is false. (Not to be confused with political libertarianism.)
Consequence argument
Van Inwagen's argument that if determinism is true, our acts are consequences of the laws and remote past, which are not up to us.
Frankfurt case
A thought experiment (Frankfurt 1969) in which an agent could not have done otherwise but seems morally responsible, used against the principle of alternative possibilities.
A-theory of time
The view that there is an objective present and that time genuinely passes; tensed facts are fundamental.
B-theory of time
The view that all times are equally real and ordered by "earlier than"; there is no objective present or flow.
Possible world
A complete way things could have been; used to analyse necessity and possibility.
Modal realism
David Lewis's view that other possible worlds are concrete and as real as ours.

Exercises

Exercise 12.1

Translate into first-order logic and say what each sentence is ontologically committed to (on Quine's criterion): (a) Some philosophers admire only logicians. (b) There is a number greater than every prime. (c) Holmes does not exist.

Show solution

(a) ∃x(Px ∧ ∀y(Axy → Ly)): philosophers (and, if there are any admired, logicians). (b) ∃x(Nx ∧ ∀y(Py → Gxy)): numbers. (It is false, since there are infinitely many primes, but its commitments are to numbers.) (c) ¬∃x Hx, with Hx: x is Holmes (or a Russellian description): no commitment to Holmes.

Exercise 12.4

Describe a Frankfurt case and explain what it is supposed to show. Then state one objection.

Show solution

See the text for the case. It is meant to show that moral responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise (PAP is false), which would undercut one route to incompatibilism. The best-known objection is the dilemma defence (Kane, David Widerker): if Jones's decision is undetermined, Black's device cannot know in advance what Jones will do, so it cannot prevent alternatives without intervening; if it is determined by a prior sign, the case assumes determinism and so begs the question against the incompatibilist.

Exercise 12.5

Explain the pre-emption problem for the counterfactual theory of causation, using an example of your own.

Show solution

Example: two assassins; the first poisons the victim's drink, and the victim dies; had the first not done so, the backup assassin would have shot him. The first assassin caused the death, but the death does not counterfactually depend on the poisoning (he would have died anyway). So counterfactual dependence is not necessary for causation, and the simple theory needs refinement.
