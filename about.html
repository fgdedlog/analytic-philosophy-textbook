About this book

This is an introductory textbook for first-year university students. It aims to give a fair, accurate picture of analytic philosophy as a whole while giving a full, practical training in deductive (symbolic) logic, which takes up roughly the middle half of the book.

Who it is for

No previous philosophy or mathematics is assumed. The logic chapters start from scratch, and every technical term is defined when first used (and collected in the glossary). The book follows British spelling and conventions.

How the book is organised

  • Part I (Chapters 1–2) explains what analytic philosophy is and introduces the basic vocabulary of argument: validity, soundness, counterexamples and fallacies.
  • Part II (Chapters 3–9) is the core. It teaches propositional and predicate logic — translation, truth tables, models and a Fitch-style natural-deduction system — and then surveys metalogic and non-classical logics. Work through Chapters 3–7 in order; each relies on the one before.
  • Part III (Chapters 10–16) introduces the main areas of analytic philosophy. These chapters can be read in any order, but each one uses the logical tools of Part II to set out key arguments precisely, so you will get most from them after Chapters 3–6.

Every chapter opens with learning objectives and closes with a summary, a key terms box and exercises. Solutions are hidden until you choose to reveal them: attempt each exercise first. All exercises and answers are also gathered on the answers page.

The interactive features

  • Truth-table generator and equivalence checker for propositional logic.
  • Fitch proof checker for natural deduction in propositional and predicate logic, with pre-loaded exercises.
  • Quick-check quizzes in the chapters (and all together on the practice page). Translation exercises accept any answer that is logically equivalent to the model answer.
  • Dotted-underlined terms show a definition when you hover over them or tab to them.
  • Search across the whole book.
  • A progress tracker: press “Mark chapter as complete” at the end of a chapter. Progress and the light/dark setting are stored only in your own browser (localStorage); nothing is sent anywhere.

Everything works offline: the site uses no external fonts, scripts or trackers. Pages print cleanly (navigation and quizzes are hidden). To print a chapter with its solutions, tick “Include solutions when printing this page” just above the chapter's exercises (or on the answers page) before printing.

How the logic was checked

Every truth table in the book is generated by the same program that runs the truth-table tool, and every proof displayed in Fitch format — in the text and in the solutions — has been checked line by line by the proof checker when the site was built. Every counter-model has been evaluated by computer, and claims such as “these two formulas are equivalent” have been verified by truth table. That does not make the book error-free, but it does mean the formal material has been mechanically checked, not just proofread.

Conventions

  • Lower-case p, q, r, … (and sometimes capitals) are sentence letters; capital letters A, B, C are also used schematically for “any formula” when stating rules.
  • In predicate logic, a–s are names, t, u, w, x, y, z are variables, and capitals are predicates.
  • The natural-deduction system is modelled on the widely used Fitch-style system found in forall x (Magnus and Button, Cambridge version); the rule names will be familiar to readers of that text.
