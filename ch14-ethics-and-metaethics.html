Ethics and metaethics

Learning objectives

  • distinguish normative ethics from metaethics;
  • explain consequentialism, deontology and virtue ethics, with a standard objection to each;
  • formalise Singer's famine argument and analyse trolley cases as an inconsistent set of principles;
  • explain Hume's is–ought gap and represent it as a claim about validity;
  • state Moore's open question argument and assess it;
  • explain emotivism and prescriptivism, and the Frege–Geach problem in terms of logical validity;
  • contrast moral realism with error theory and quasi-realism.

Ethics divides into three layers. Applied ethics asks particular questions: is euthanasia permissible? What do we owe to animals, or to distant strangers? Normative ethics asks what makes right acts right in general. Metaethics asks what moral claims are: can they be true or false? Are there moral facts? What do moral words mean? Logic has a part at every level: in assessing arguments in applied ethics, in testing principles against cases, and in metaethics, where the logical behaviour of moral sentences becomes evidence about their meaning.

Consequentialism

Consequentialism holds that the rightness of an act depends only on its consequences. The best-known form is utilitarianism (Jeremy Bentham, An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, 1789; John Stuart Mill, Utilitarianism, 1863): an act is right iff it produces at least as much total well-being as any alternative available to the agent. Classical utilitarians were hedonists about well-being: what is good is pleasure and the absence of pain. Mill distinguished higher pleasures (of the intellect, feeling and imagination) from lower ones: “It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.”

Attractions: impartiality (each counts for one), concern with what actually matters to people, and a single decision procedure. Objections:

  • Justice: a sheriff can prevent a lethal riot by framing and executing an innocent man; utilitarianism seems to require it. (Variants of this case are due to H. J. McCloskey, 1957.)
  • Integrity and separateness of persons (Bernard Williams, in Utilitarianism: For and Against, 1973, with J. J. C. Smart): utilitarianism treats agents as channels for producing the best state of affairs, alienating them from their own projects; and (John Rawls, A Theory of Justice, 1971) it “does not take seriously the distinction between persons”, permitting one person's suffering to be outweighed by others' gains.
  • Hedonism: Robert Nozick's experience machine (Anarchy, State, and Utopia, 1974) would give you any experiences you desire — writing a great novel, making friends — while you float in a tank. Most people would not plug in, suggesting that we value more than experiences.
  • Demandingness: as long as others are starving, you should give away almost everything.

Rule consequentialism (Brad Hooker, Ideal Code, Real World, 2000) evaluates rules rather than acts: an act is right iff it accords with the rules whose general acceptance would have the best consequences. A rule allowing sheriffs to frame innocents would be disastrous if generally accepted, so the view avoids the justice objection — though critics ask why one should follow the rule in the rare case where breaking it is better.

A worked argument: Singer on famine

Peter Singer (“Famine, Affluence, and Morality”, 1972) argues that if you would wade into a shallow pond to save a drowning child, ruining your clothes, you should also give money to prevent deaths from famine. His core argument:

  1. P1Suffering and death from lack of food, shelter and medical care are bad.
  2. P2If it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do it.
  3. P3It is in our power to prevent some such suffering and death without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance.
  4. CWe ought to prevent some such suffering and death.

The argument is valid, and Singer notes that P2 is not even specifically utilitarian — a deontologist could accept it. So a critic must challenge a premise: perhaps proximity or the presence of others who could help reduces my obligation; perhaps P2 is too demanding; or perhaps the empirical premise P3 is uncertain (does aid work?). Formalising forces the critic to say exactly which premise is at fault.

Deontology

Deontology holds that some acts are required or forbidden independently of their consequences: there are duties (not to lie, not to kill the innocent, to keep promises) and rights that constrain the pursuit of good outcomes. The most influential deontologist is Immanuel Kant (Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, 1785).

Kant distinguishes hypothetical imperatives (“if you want to be healthy, exercise”), which bind only those with the relevant desires, from the categorical imperative, which binds all rational agents unconditionally. Its two most discussed formulations:

  • Formula of universal law: “act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law.” Kant's example: a lying promise to repay a loan. If everyone made such promises, no one would believe them, so the maxim cannot be universalised without contradiction.
  • Formula of humanity: “act so that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means.” The framed innocent is used merely as a means.

Objections: absolutism — Kant held that you must not lie even to a murderer asking where your friend is hiding (“On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy”, 1797); conflicts of duties; and the difficulty of specifying maxims (any act can be described by a maxim that universalises). W. D. Ross (The Right and the Good, 1930) proposed a plurality of prima facie duties — fidelity, non-maleficence, beneficence, justice and others — which can be outweighed by one another, with no algorithm for weighing them.

Virtue ethics

Virtue ethics makes character, rather than acts or rules, fundamental. Its source is Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics: the good life for a human being is eudaimonia (flourishing), activity in accordance with the virtues; and each virtue (courage, generosity, temperance) is a stable disposition to feel and act well, typically a mean between two vices (courage between cowardice and rashness), acquired by habituation and guided by practical wisdom (phronesis). G. E. M. Anscombe's “Modern Moral Philosophy” (1958) argued that moral philosophy should abandon the notion of moral obligation, a relic of divine-law ethics, and return to Aristotelian virtue concepts; this helped revive virtue ethics. Rosalind Hursthouse (On Virtue Ethics, 1999) proposes: an act is right iff it is what a virtuous agent would characteristically do in the circumstances.

Objections: it gives too little action guidance (what would the virtuous person do?); and some situationist psychologists and philosophers (John Doris, Gilbert Harman) argue that experiments show behaviour depends far more on situation than on stable character traits.

Testing principles: the trolley problem

Philippa Foot (“The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect”, 1967) introduced a runaway tram heading towards five workers; the driver can steer onto a side track where it will kill one. Most people judge that diverting is permissible. Judith Jarvis Thomson (“The Trolley Problem”, 1985) added the footbridge case: you can stop the trolley only by pushing a large man off a bridge into its path, killing him but saving the five. Most people judge this impermissible. The trolley problem is to find a principle that explains the difference.

The method is logical. Suppose we hold three things: (1) in Switch, diverting is permissible; (2) in Footbridge, pushing is impermissible; (3) a principle P — say, “whenever you can save five by an act that kills one, the act is permissible” — which applies to both cases. With s: diverting is permissible; f: pushing is permissible; and P instantiated to the two cases, the set is inconsistent:

More carefully: P implies s ∧ f, and with ¬f that is a contradiction. So we must either give up a case judgement or find a principle that distinguishes the cases. Candidates:

  • The doctrine of double effect (traced to Aquinas): it can be permissible to bring about a bad effect as a foreseen side effect of pursuing a good end, but not as a means to it. In Switch, the one's death is a side effect; in Footbridge, his body is the means.
  • Doing versus allowing; or Kant's prohibition on using persons merely as means.
  • Thomson's loop case (the side track loops back to the five, so the one's body is needed to stop the trolley) was designed to test double effect: many think diverting is still permissible, although the one is now used as a means.

Joshua Greene's neuroimaging work (2001 onwards) suggested that “personal” harms such as pushing trigger emotional responses, which some (including Greene and Singer) take to debunk the Footbridge intuition, and others take to vindicate it.

Metaethics

Hume's is–ought gap

Hume (Treatise, 3.1.1) observed that moralists move, imperceptibly, from propositions connected by “is” to propositions connected by “ought”, and asked how this new relation “can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it”. Understood as a logical point, the is–ought gap says that no set of purely descriptive premises entails an evaluative conclusion. In propositional terms, with d descriptive and o evaluative:

The bare inference fails (the second row is a counterexample); adding the bridging premise d → o, which is itself evaluative, makes it valid. Logic alone guarantees this much for any distinct atoms. Whether there are non-logical, meaning-based routes from is to ought is disputed: John Searle (“How to Derive ‘Ought’ from ‘Is’”, 1964) argued that from “Jones uttered the words ‘I hereby promise to pay you five dollars’” we can derive, via institutional facts about promising, that Jones ought to pay. Critics reply that an evaluative premise (that one ought to keep promises) has been smuggled in.

Moore's open question argument

G. E. Moore (Principia Ethica, 1903) argued that goodness cannot be identified with any natural property. Take any proposed definition, say “good” = “pleasant”. If it were correct, the question “I grant that X is pleasant, but is it good?” would be as closed as “I grant that X is a bachelor, but is he unmarried?” But the first question remains open — a competent speaker can sensibly ask it. So “good” does not mean “pleasant”; and the same applies to any natural property. To identify goodness with a natural property is to commit what Moore called the naturalistic fallacy. This is the open question argument.

Look at the logical form: “Goodness = pleasantness; it is not an open question whether pleasant things are pleasant; so it is not an open question whether pleasant things are good.” This applies Leibniz's Law inside an intensional context (“it is an open question whether …”) — the same structure as the masked man fallacy (Chapter 13). Water is H₂O, yet “this is water, but is it H₂O?” was once an open question. So the argument at most shows that “good” is not synonymous with any naturalistic expression — not that goodness is not a natural property. This reply is central to “Cornell realism” (Richard Boyd, Nicholas Sturgeon, David Brink), which holds that moral properties are natural properties discovered a posteriori.

Non-cognitivism: emotivism and prescriptivism

Moore concluded that goodness is a simple non-natural property known by intuition. Many found that mysterious. A. J. Ayer (Language, Truth and Logic, 1936, ch. 6) proposed instead that moral sentences do not state facts at all: “Stealing money is wrong” expresses disapproval — roughly, “Stealing money!!” said in a peculiar tone of horror. Moral judgements are not true or false. This is emotivism, developed in more detail by C. L. Stevenson (Ethics and Language, 1944). It neatly explains the open question (no factual definition could capture an expression of attitude) and the motivational force of moral judgement.

R. M. Hare (The Language of Morals, 1952) offered prescriptivism: moral judgements are prescriptions, like commands, but universalisable — if I judge that I ought to do X in this situation, I am committed to prescribing that anyone exactly similarly situated do X. Hare argued that imperatives have a logic too (“take all the boxes to the station; this is one of the boxes; so take this to the station”).

Today's heirs are called expressivism (Simon Blackburn, Allan Gibbard).

The Frege–Geach problem

Peter Geach (“Assertion”, 1965), building on a point of Frege's, raised a problem that turns on logical validity. Consider:

  1. P1Lying is wrong.
  2. P2If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong.
  3. CGetting your little brother to lie is wrong.

This is an instance of modus ponens, and it is valid only if “lying is wrong” means the same in P1 and in the antecedent of P2. But in P2 nobody is expressing disapproval of lying — one can assert P2 while thinking lying is fine. If the meaning of “lying is wrong” is the expression of an attitude, it seems to have a different meaning in the two premises, and the argument would equivocate. Yet it is plainly valid. So expressivists owe an account of the meaning of moral sentences in unasserted contexts — conditionals, negations, questions — that explains why such arguments are valid. This is the Frege–Geach problem. Blackburn (Spreading the Mind, 1984) suggested that P2 expresses a higher-order attitude (disapproval of the combination of disapproving of lying and not disapproving of getting others to lie); critics argue that this explains at most a kind of inconsistency of attitudes, not logical validity. The debate continues (Mark Schroeder, Being For, 2008).

Realism and error theory

Moral realism holds that there are objective moral facts, which moral judgements aim to describe and which hold independently of anyone's attitudes. Realists may be non-naturalists (Moore; recently Derek Parfit, On What Matters, 2011; Russ Shafer-Landau) or naturalists (Cornell realism).

J. L. Mackie (Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong, 1977) agreed with the realist about what moral judgements mean — they purport to state objective, categorically binding facts — but argued that there are no such facts, so all positive moral judgements are false. This is error theory. His arguments: from relativity (moral disagreement across cultures is best explained by differing ways of life, not by differing perception of objective values) and from queerness (objective values would be “entities or qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe”, requiring a special faculty to detect them).

Blackburn's quasi-realism aims to earn, on expressivist foundations, the right to all the realist-sounding things we say — that moral claims are true, that some are mind-independent (“kicking dogs would be wrong even if we approved of it”), that we can be mistaken — without commitment to moral facts as the realist understands them.

Summary

  • Normative ethics asks what makes acts right; metaethics asks what moral claims are.
  • Consequentialism judges acts by outcomes; objections concern justice, integrity, hedonism and demandingness. Singer's famine argument is valid, so critics must reject a premise.
  • Kantian deontology tests maxims by the categorical imperative; Ross offers plural prima facie duties.
  • Virtue ethics makes character and flourishing basic.
  • Trolley cases show an inconsistency between intuitive case judgements and simple principles; double effect is one proposed resolution.
  • The is–ought gap is a validity claim: descriptive premises alone do not entail evaluative conclusions.
  • Moore's open question argument shows at most non-synonymy, since it applies Leibniz's Law in an intensional context.
  • Emotivism and prescriptivism deny that moral judgements state facts; the Frege–Geach problem asks how they then explain valid moral arguments.
  • Realists accept moral facts; Mackie's error theory says moral claims are all false; quasi-realism seeks realist talk without realist metaphysics.

Key terms

Normative ethics
The part of ethics that asks what makes actions right or wrong and what makes lives or characters good.
Metaethics
The study of the nature, meaning and status of moral claims.
Consequentialism
The view that the rightness of an action depends only on the value of its consequences.
Utilitarianism
The consequentialist view that the right act is the one that maximises overall well-being or happiness.
Rule consequentialism
The view that an act is right iff it is permitted by the set of rules whose general acceptance would have the best consequences.
Experience machine
Nozick's thought experiment about a machine giving any experiences you like, used against hedonism.
Deontology
The view that some actions are required or forbidden independently of their consequences, e.g. by duties or rights.
Hypothetical imperative
A command that applies only given a desire or end ("If you want X, do Y").
Categorical imperative
Kant's supreme principle of morality: act only on maxims you could will to be universal laws (among other formulations).
Virtue ethics
The approach to ethics that takes character and the virtues as central.
Trolley problem
A family of cases testing intuitions about killing, letting die and the doctrine of double effect.
Doctrine of double effect
The principle that it can be permissible to bring about a harm as a foreseen side effect that it would be wrong to intend.
Is–ought gap
Hume's thesis that no conclusion about what ought to be follows from premises solely about what is.
Open question argument
Moore's argument that "good" cannot be defined as any natural property N, since "Is N good?" always remains open.
Naturalistic fallacy
Moore's name for the alleged error of identifying goodness with a natural property.
Emotivism
The metaethical view (Ayer, Stevenson) that moral utterances express emotions or attitudes rather than state facts.
Prescriptivism
Hare's view that moral judgements are universalisable prescriptions, akin to commands.
Expressivism
The metaethical view that moral judgements express non-cognitive attitudes rather than describe moral facts.
Frege–Geach problem
The problem for expressivism of explaining the meaning of moral sentences in unasserted contexts such as the antecedents of conditionals, and so the validity of moral arguments.
Moral realism
The view that there are objective moral facts that make moral judgements true or false.
Error theory
Mackie's view that moral judgements purport to state objective facts but are all false, since there are no such facts.
Quasi-realism
Blackburn's programme of explaining, on expressivist foundations, why we are entitled to talk as if there were moral truths.

Exercises

Exercise 14.1

Which premise of Singer's argument would each critic reject? (a) “Famine relief often does more harm than good.” (b) “I have special obligations to my family that are of comparable moral importance to strangers' lives.” (c) “Morality cannot require us to do more than our fair share.”

Show solution

(a) P3 (that we can prevent the bad without comparable sacrifice — here, that we can prevent it at all). (b) P3 as applied to large donations: what is sacrificed is of comparable moral importance — or a restriction of P2. (c) P2: the obligation to prevent bad outcomes is limited to a fair share, not unlimited.

Exercise 14.3

Explain why the open question argument resembles the masked man fallacy. Does this show that Moore was wrong?

Show solution

Both apply Leibniz's Law to properties that depend on how something is thought of (“being an open question whether…”, “being doubted by me”). Since “water = H₂O” was a substantive discovery while its openness was real, openness cannot show non-identity of properties. This shows the argument doesn't establish that goodness is not a natural property, though it may establish that “good” is not analytically definable in natural terms — a weaker but still interesting conclusion. Some defenders reply that Moore was interested in analysis (definition) anyway.

Exercise 14.4

Construct a valid moral argument using modus tollens and explain the Frege–Geach problem for it.

Show solution

E.g. “If eating meat is wrong, then factory farming is wrong. Factory farming is not wrong. So eating meat is not wrong.” Valid by MT. The expressivist must explain the meaning of “eating meat is wrong” in the antecedent (no disapproval expressed) and of “factory farming is not wrong” (what attitude does a negated moral sentence express — tolerance? not disapproving?), in a way that makes the argument valid rather than equivocal.

Exercise 14.5

“The doctrine of double effect solves the trolley problem.” Discuss, using at least the switch, footbridge and loop cases.

Show solution

A strong answer explains DDE, shows how it distinguishes Switch (death a side effect) from Footbridge (death/body a means), then presents Loop as a case where DDE seems to give the wrong verdict (if diverting in Loop is permissible), and considers responses (e.g. Thomson's later change of view in “Turning the Trolley”, 2008, where she argued that even the bystander at the switch may not divert). It should reach a reasoned conclusion.
