Learning objectives

  • identify the premises and conclusion of an argument in ordinary prose;
  • distinguish deductive, inductive and abductive reasoning;
  • define validity, soundness, inductive strength and cogency, and apply them to examples;
  • reconstruct an argument in standard premise–conclusion form, supplying missing premises;
  • use the counterexample method to show an argument form invalid;
  • recognise common informal fallacies.

What is an argument?

In philosophy an argument is not a quarrel. It is a set of statements, called premises, offered as reasons for believing a further statement, the conclusion. Premises and conclusions are propositions: things that can be true or false. Questions, commands and exclamations are not propositions (though they can sometimes be rephrased as such).

Arguments usually come wrapped in prose, and the first skill is to unwrap them. Indicator words help:

Conclusion indicatorsPremise indicators
therefore, so, hence, thus, consequently, it follows that, which shows that, we may conclude thatbecause, since, for, given that, as, the reason is that, in view of the fact that

Indicators are not infallible (“since” can mean “from the time that”), and many arguments have none. Ask: what is the author trying to get me to believe, and what reasons are offered?

Example

“We cannot know that the future will resemble the past, since any argument for it would have to assume it.”

Conclusion: we cannot know that the future will resemble the past. Premise: any argument that the future will resemble the past would have to assume it. (There is also an unstated premise: that an argument that assumes its conclusion cannot give us knowledge of it. We return to unstated premises below.)

Not every passage containing “because” is an argument. “The bridge collapsed because the bolts corroded” is an explanation: it takes the collapse for granted and says why it happened. An argument tries to show that something is true; an explanation tries to show why something we already accept is true.

Three kinds of reasoning

Deduction

In a deductive argument, the premises are intended to guarantee the conclusion: if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true.

  1. P1All humans are mortal.
  2. P2Socrates is a human.
  3. CSocrates is mortal.

Induction

In an inductive argument, the premises are intended to make the conclusion probable, not certain. Typical inductive arguments generalise from samples or predict from past patterns.

  1. P1Every raven observed so far, in many places and over many years, has been black.
  2. CThe next raven observed will be black.

Even if the premise is true, the conclusion could be false. Inductive arguments are assessed by their strength: how probable the premises make the conclusion. The philosophical problem of justifying induction at all is Hume's problem (Chapter 11).

Abduction

Abduction, or inference to the best explanation, reasons from data to the hypothesis that would best explain them.

  1. P1The biscuit tin is empty, there are crumbs on the sofa, and the dog looks guilty.
  2. P2The best explanation of these facts is that the dog ate the biscuits.
  3. CThe dog ate the biscuits.

What makes an explanation “best” is debated: simplicity, scope, fit with background knowledge and lack of ad hoc assumptions are common criteria. Much scientific reasoning, and much philosophical reasoning, is abductive. Some philosophers treat abduction as a species of induction; others treat it as a distinct form.

Quick check “Every swan I have seen is white, so all swans are white.” What kind of argument is this?

It generalises from observed cases, so it is inductive. (It is famously not deductively valid: black swans exist in Australia.)

Validity and soundness

The central concept of deductive logic is validity.

Definition. An argument is valid if and only if it is impossible for all its premises to be true and its conclusion false. Equivalently: in every possible situation in which the premises are true, the conclusion is true too. An argument that is not valid is invalid.

Validity is about the connection between premises and conclusion, not about whether the premises are actually true. These arguments are valid:

  1. P1All fish can fly.
  2. P2Salmon are fish.
  3. CSalmon can fly.
  1. P1If it is raining, the ground is wet.
  2. P2It is raining.
  3. CThe ground is wet.

The first has a false premise and a false conclusion, but it is valid: if the premises were true, the conclusion would have to be. Conversely, an argument with true premises and a true conclusion can be invalid:

  1. P1Paris is in France.
  2. CWater is H₂O.

Here the premise gives no support at all; it is possible (in the sense relevant to logic) for Paris to be in France and water not to be H₂O.

A sound argument is a valid argument whose premises are all true. A sound argument must have a true conclusion. So there are two ways to criticise a deductive argument: show it is invalid, or show that a premise is false.

True conclusionFalse conclusion
Valid, all premises truepossible (sound)impossible
Valid, some premise falsepossiblepossible
Invalidpossiblepossible

The only combination ruled out is a valid argument with true premises and a false conclusion. That is the whole point of validity: it is truth-preserving.

For inductive arguments, the parallel notions are strength (the premises make the conclusion probable) and cogency (strong, and with true premises). Unlike validity, strength comes in degrees, and adding premises can weaken an inductive argument (“…but this raven comes from a population with an albino mutation”). Adding premises can never make a valid argument invalid.

Quick check An argument has true premises and a false conclusion. What follows?

A valid argument cannot have true premises and a false conclusion. So any argument that actually does is invalid.

Quick check An argument has false premises and a true conclusion. What follows about its validity?

Validity rules out only true premises with a false conclusion. False premises with a true conclusion are compatible with both validity and invalidity.

Logical form

Why are some arguments valid? Compare:

  1. P1If Ada is in Leeds, she is in Yorkshire.
  2. P2Ada is in Leeds.
  3. CAda is in Yorkshire.
  1. P1If the number is prime and greater than 2, it is odd.
  2. P2The number is prime and greater than 2.
  3. CThe number is odd.

They share a pattern: If A then B; A; therefore B. Every argument with this pattern is valid, whatever A and B are. This pattern is called modus ponens. The pattern is the argument's logical form, and formal logic is the study of which forms are valid. That is why logic can be done with symbols: the content does not matter, only the form.

Some important forms, valid and invalid:

NameFormVerdict
Modus ponensIf A then B; A; so Bvalid
Modus tollensIf A then B; not B; so not Avalid
Disjunctive syllogismA or B; not A; so Bvalid
Hypothetical syllogismIf A then B; if B then C; so if A then Cvalid
Affirming the consequentIf A then B; B; so Ainvalid
Denying the antecedentIf A then B; not A; so not Binvalid

In Chapter 4 you will be able to prove each of these verdicts mechanically.

The counterexample method

To show that an argument form is invalid, find a counterexample: an argument of the same form with obviously true premises and an obviously false conclusion. Since a valid form can never take you from truths to a falsehood, one such instance shows the form is invalid.

Example: affirming the consequent

“If the Prime Minister is secretly a lizard, she is a vertebrate. She is a vertebrate. So she is secretly a lizard.” Is the form valid? Substitute: “If Rex is a cat, Rex is a mammal. Rex is a mammal. So Rex is a cat.” Take Rex to be a dog: the premises are true and the conclusion false. So the form If A then B; B; so A is invalid.

The method has limits. Failing to find a counterexample does not prove validity — you may not have been imaginative enough. And one argument can be an instance of several forms: every argument is an instance of the invalid form “P; so Q”, but that does not make every argument invalid. An argument is valid if it has some valid form. Formal logic (Part II) gives precise, mechanical tests that overcome these limits for the arguments it can represent.

Reconstructing arguments

To evaluate an argument, first put it in standard form: a numbered list of premises followed by the conclusion. The steps:

  1. Find the conclusion. Write it last.
  2. Find the stated premises. Rewrite each as a complete, self-contained declarative sentence. Remove rhetoric, repetition and examples that are merely illustrative.
  3. Use terms consistently: if the author says “free”, “unconstrained” and “at liberty” for the same idea, pick one.
  4. Supply missing premises needed to make the argument valid — but choose the most plausible ones the author could accept (the principle of charity).
  5. If the argument has stages, mark intermediate conclusions (“C1”) that serve as premises for later steps.

An argument with an unstated premise or conclusion is an enthymeme. Most everyday arguments are enthymemes, and much philosophical criticism consists in making a hidden premise explicit and showing that it is false.

Worked reconstruction

Passage: “Eating meat cannot be wrong. After all, humans have always eaten meat, and our ancestors would not have survived without it.”

  1. P1Humans have always eaten meat.
  2. P2Our ancestors would not have survived without eating meat.
  3. P3[unstated] If a practice has always been followed by humans and was necessary for our ancestors' survival, then it is not wrong now.
  4. CEating meat is not wrong.

With P3 the argument is valid; without it, it is not. But P3 is very doubtful: practices that were once necessary may cease to be so, and long tradition does not make something right (compare the appeal to tradition fallacy below). Making P3 explicit shows exactly where the argument is weakest.

Quick check “Abortion is the killing of an innocent human being, so it is wrong.” Which unstated premise makes this valid?

The conclusion follows validly with “All killing of innocent human beings is wrong”. (Critics typically challenge either this premise or the claim that a foetus is a human being in the morally relevant sense.)

Informal fallacies

A fallacy is a common pattern of bad reasoning. Formal fallacies are invalid forms like affirming the consequent. Informal fallacies are failures that depend on content or context. Here are the most important. (Note that many of these patterns are not always bad; the skill is to tell when they are.)

Ad hominem
Attacking the person rather than the argument. “Smith's argument for the tax is worthless — he is a known liar.” (Whether an argument is good does not depend on who gives it — though a person's reliability can matter when we are taking their testimony on trust.)
Strawman
Attacking a distorted or weaker version of a view. “Evolutionists think we are descended from chimpanzees, which is absurd.”
Appeal to authority
Citing someone who is not a genuine authority on the matter, or treating authority as decisive where reasons are needed.
Appeal to popularity (ad populum)
“Most people believe it, so it is true.”
Appeal to tradition / nature
“It has always been done” or “it is natural”, therefore it is good.
Begging the question
Assuming what is to be proved. “God exists because the Bible says so, and the Bible is true because it is the word of God.” (In ordinary English, “begs the question” is often used to mean “raises the question”; in philosophy it means circular reasoning.)
Equivocation
Using a word in two senses: “Only man is rational. No woman is a man. So no woman is rational.” (“Man” = human beings / male human beings.)
False dilemma
Presenting two options as the only ones when there are more. “Either we ban cars or the planet is doomed.”
Slippery slope
Claiming without adequate support that a first step will lead to a chain of increasingly bad consequences.
Hasty generalisation
Generalising from too small or unrepresentative a sample.
Post hoc ergo propter hoc
“After this, therefore because of this”: inferring causation from mere temporal succession.
Composition and division
Inferring that a whole has a property because its parts do (composition), or that parts have a property because the whole does (division). “Every brick is light, so the wall is light.”
Red herring
Introducing an irrelevant topic to divert attention.
Appeal to ignorance
“No one has proved it false, so it is true” (or vice versa).

Quick check “Professor Jones says free will is an illusion, but she would say that — she's a determinist.” Which fallacy is this?

It dismisses the claim by pointing to features of the person who makes it, rather than engaging with her reasons.

Summary

  • An argument is a set of premises offered in support of a conclusion; it differs from an explanation.
  • Deductive arguments aim at guaranteeing their conclusions; inductive ones at making them probable; abductive ones infer the best explanation.
  • An argument is valid if it is impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false; sound if also all its premises are true. Inductive arguments are strong or weak; cogent if strong with true premises.
  • Validity depends on logical form. Modus ponens, modus tollens, disjunctive syllogism and hypothetical syllogism are valid; affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent are not.
  • A counterexample — same form, true premises, false conclusion — shows a form invalid.
  • Reconstruct arguments in standard form, supplying charitable missing premises. Watch for informal fallacies.

Key terms

Argument
A set of statements (premises) offered in support of a further statement (the conclusion).
Premise
A statement offered as a reason in an argument.
Conclusion
The statement an argument tries to establish.
Deduction
Reasoning in which the conclusion is meant to follow necessarily from the premises.
Induction
Reasoning in which the premises make the conclusion probable but do not guarantee it, e.g. generalising from observed cases.
Abduction
Inference to the best explanation: reasoning from evidence to the hypothesis that would best explain it.
Valid
(Of an argument) such that it is impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false.
Sound argument
A valid argument with all true premises.
Cogent
(Of an inductive argument) strong and with all premises true.
Logical form
The structure of a sentence or argument relevant to validity, abstracting from subject matter.
Counterexample
A case in which an argument's premises are true and its conclusion false, showing the argument (form) invalid.
Enthymeme
An argument with an unstated premise or conclusion.
Fallacy
A common pattern of bad reasoning.
Modus ponens
The valid form "If A then B; A; therefore B" (→E).
Modus tollens
The valid form "If A then B; not B; therefore not A".
Begging the question
Assuming, in the premises, the very thing the argument is meant to establish (circular reasoning).

Exercises

Exercise 2.1

Identify the premises and conclusion: “Since the mind can doubt the existence of the body but cannot doubt its own existence, the mind is not the same thing as the body.”

Show solution

P1: I can doubt that my body exists. P2: I cannot doubt that my mind exists. C: My mind is not the same thing as my body. (This is a version of an argument associated with Descartes. To be valid it needs a premise like “If x and y are identical, whatever is true of x is true of y” — Leibniz's Law — and it is controversial whether “being doubted by me” is the kind of property to which that law applies. See Chapter 13.)

Exercise 2.2

True or false? Explain each answer. (a) Every sound argument is valid. (b) Every valid argument is sound. (c) A valid argument can have a false conclusion. (d) If an argument has a false conclusion, at least one premise is false. (e) An invalid argument can have true premises and a true conclusion.

Show solution

(a) True: soundness is defined as validity plus true premises. (b) False: a valid argument may have false premises. (c) True, provided at least one premise is false. (d) False in general; it holds only for valid arguments. An invalid argument can have true premises and a false conclusion. (e) True — e.g. “Paris is in France; so water is H₂O.”

Exercise 2.3

Use the counterexample method to show that denying the antecedent (If A then B; not A; so not B) is invalid.

Show solution

“If Rex is a cat, Rex is a mammal. Rex is not a cat. So Rex is not a mammal.” If Rex is a dog, both premises are true and the conclusion false. So the form is invalid.

Exercise 2.4

Reconstruct in standard form, supplying any missing premise: “You shouldn't trust that newspaper — it's owned by a billionaire.”

Show solution

P1: The newspaper is owned by a billionaire. P2 (unstated): No newspaper owned by a billionaire should be trusted. C: You should not trust the newspaper. P2 is very strong and probably false as stated; a more charitable version might be “Newspapers owned by billionaires have an incentive to slant coverage in their owners' interests, so their reporting on those interests should be checked against other sources” — which supports a much weaker conclusion.

Exercise 2.5

Name the fallacy (if any) in each: (a) “Nobody has ever shown that ghosts don't exist, so they do.” (b) “Each player is excellent, so the team must be excellent.” (c) “Either you support this law or you don't care about children.” (d) “After I started wearing my lucky socks we won three matches, so the socks work.”

Show solution

(a) Appeal to ignorance. (b) Composition — excellent individuals may play badly together. (c) False dilemma. (d) Post hoc ergo propter hoc (and hasty generalisation from three cases).

Exercise 2.6

Explain the difference between an argument and an explanation, using an example of each that contains the word “because”.

Show solution

Argument: “The suspect cannot have done it, because she was in Glasgow at the time.” The speaker is trying to convince us that the suspect did not do it, giving a reason. Explanation: “The ice melted because the temperature rose above 0 °C.” Here the melting is taken for granted and the speaker says what caused it. Test: is the “conclusion” already accepted (explanation) or in dispute (argument)?
