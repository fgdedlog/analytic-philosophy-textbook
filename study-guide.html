Study guide: how to study philosophy and write a philosophy essay

Philosophy is learned by doing it: reconstructing arguments, testing them, and defending a view of your own in clear prose. This guide covers how to use the book, how to read philosophy, how to tackle logic problems, and how to plan and write an essay.

Using this book

  • Logic is cumulative. Work through Chapters 3–7 in order, and do the exercises as you go. Skimming a proof is not the same as being able to produce one. Aim to do at least half the exercises in each logic chapter before moving on.
  • Attempt before revealing. Solutions are hidden by default. Give each exercise a genuine attempt — even a wrong one teaches you where your understanding breaks down — then check.
  • Use the tools to check, not to think for you. The truth-table generator and proof checker give instant feedback. Write a proof on paper first, then enter it to check each line.
  • Little and often. Thirty minutes of logic practice on five days beats one long session. Use the practice page for quick revision.
  • Read Part III chapters actively. For each argument, ask: what is the conclusion? What are the premises? Is it valid? Which premise is weakest?

Reading philosophy

Primary texts — the papers and books listed in Further reading — are dense. Read each one at least twice.

  1. Survey. Read the introduction and conclusion, and look at the section headings. What is the main thesis?
  2. Read for the argument. Mark where the author states the thesis, gives reasons, considers objections and replies.
  3. Reconstruct. Write the main argument in standard form (numbered premises and conclusion, as in Chapter 2). Add any unstated premises needed to make it valid.
  4. Evaluate. Is the argument valid? If so, which premise would a critic reject? Can you think of a counterexample?
  5. Summarise. Can you explain the argument in five sentences to someone who has not read it? If not, read again.

Principle of charity. Interpret an author in the way that makes their argument strongest. Criticising a weak version of a view (a straw man) proves nothing; criticising the strongest version is real progress.

Tackling logic problems

  • Translation. Identify the atomic sentences first and write a key. Find the main connective of the English sentence. Watch for “only if”, “unless”, “neither…nor” and quantifier words (see the cheat-sheet). Check your answer by reading it back into English.
  • Truth tables. Be systematic: always list rows in the same order, and work from the innermost subformulas outwards. To test validity, look only for rows where all premises are true.
  • Proofs. Work backwards from the goal. Ask: what is the main connective of what I want? To prove a conditional, assume the antecedent; to prove a negation, assume the unnegated formula and aim for ⊥; to prove a universal, prove an instance with a fresh name. Then work forwards from the premises using elimination rules. If stuck, try indirect proof (IP).
  • Invalidity. If you cannot find a proof, try building a counterexample (a row, or a model) — you may be trying to prove something false.

Writing a philosophy essay

A philosophy essay is not a report of what philosophers have said. It is an argument for a thesis of your own, which engages with others' arguments. Markers typically look for: a clear thesis; accurate exposition; critical analysis; consideration of objections; and clear, well-organised writing.

1. Understand the question

Underline the key terms. “Is knowledge justified true belief?” asks you to take a position; “Explain and assess the Gettier problem” asks for exposition and evaluation. If a term is ambiguous (e.g. “free”), say which sense you mean.

2. Plan

Decide your thesis before you write. A plan might be:

  1. Introduction (about 10%): the question, why it matters, your thesis, and a road map. (“I will argue that … First I … Then I consider the objection that … I reply that …”)
  2. Exposition (25–30%): state the view or argument you are assessing, clearly and charitably. Put key arguments in standard form.
  3. Critical discussion (40–50%): your argument. Raise an objection; consider the best reply; assess it. Depth beats breadth — two objections examined thoroughly are worth more than six listed.
  4. Conclusion (about 10%): what you have shown, and its limits. Don't introduce new material.

3. Write clearly

  • Use plain, precise language and short sentences. Philosophy is hard enough without difficult prose.
  • Define technical terms when you first use them, and use each term in one sense throughout.
  • Signpost: “The first premise is…”, “One might object that…”, “This reply fails because…”.
  • Use examples, including thought experiments, to make abstract points concrete.
  • Use the first person to state your view (“I will argue…”) — this is normal in philosophy.
  • Avoid rhetorical questions, appeals to dictionaries for philosophical terms, and sweeping historical openings (“Since the dawn of time…”).

4. Use logic in your essay

You will rarely need symbols in an essay, but logical thinking is essential:

  • When you present an argument, number its premises so you can refer to them (“premise 2 is false because…”).
  • If an argument is invalid, say so and show a counterexample; if it is valid, the only way to resist it is to reject a premise.
  • Distinguish necessary from sufficient conditions, and “some” from “all”.
  • Check for equivocation: is a key word used in the same sense throughout?

5. Reference properly

Cite every source you use, including for ideas you paraphrase. Follow the referencing style your department requires (often author–date, e.g. Gettier 1963). Quote sparingly: explain ideas in your own words, which shows understanding. Never present others' work, including text produced by software, as your own; check your institution's rules on academic integrity.

6. Revise

Leave time to reread the essay after a break. Does each paragraph advance the argument? Is the thesis actually defended? Read it aloud to catch unclear sentences. Check the word limit.

Example: from question to plan

Question: “Does the closure principle lead to scepticism?”

Thesis: The closure-based sceptical argument is valid, but its second premise is false: we can know that we are not brains in vats, and safety theory explains how.

  1. Introduction: closure, the sceptical argument, thesis.
  2. Exposition: the argument in standard form (Chapter 11); show it is valid (modus tollens).
  3. Option 1: deny closure (Nozick). Objection: the “abominable conjunction” — “I know I have hands but don't know I'm not a handless BIV” — sounds absurd (DeRose's phrase).
  4. Option 2: deny premise 2 via safety. Explain safety; show it makes the anti-BIV belief safe. Objection: this seems to make anti-sceptical knowledge too easy. Reply.
  5. Conclusion.

Exams

  • For logic papers, practise under timed conditions; speed comes from familiarity with standard proof patterns (Chapter 5's strategies).
  • For essay papers, prepare topics, not answers: know the main arguments, objections and replies, and be ready to apply them to the question actually set.
  • Read every question before you start, and allocate time in proportion to marks.
