Learning objectives

  • explain why falsification is deductively valid while confirmation is not, and how the Duhem–Quine thesis complicates this;
  • describe Kuhn's account of paradigms and scientific revolutions;
  • explain underdetermination and the debate between scientific realists and anti-realists;
  • state Hempel's paradox of the ravens;
  • formalise versions of the ontological and cosmological arguments and identify the quantifier shift fallacy;
  • outline the design argument and Hume's objections;
  • formalise the logical problem of evil and explain how the free will defence answers it.

This chapter covers two areas where deductive logic does a great deal of work: the philosophy of science, which asks how evidence bears on theories, and the philosophy of religion, which assesses arguments for and against the existence of God. In both, the difference between deductive validity and weaker forms of support is central.

Part A: Philosophy of science

Confirmation, induction and falsification

A scientific hypothesis h predicts an observation o: h → o. If we observe o, does that prove h? No: that would be affirming the consequent.

The third row (h false, o true) is a counterexample: the prediction can come true even though the hypothesis is false. Successful predictions confirm a hypothesis, at best inductively. But if the prediction fails, the hypothesis is refuted, by modus tollens:

Karl Popper (Logik der Forschung, 1934; English The Logic of Scientific Discovery, 1959) built a philosophy of science on this asymmetry. Since Hume showed induction cannot be justified (Chapter 11), science should not be seen as inductive at all. It proceeds by conjectures and refutations: bold hypotheses are proposed and subjected to severe tests; those that survive are corroborated, but never proven. Falsificationism also offers a criterion of demarcation: a theory is scientific iff it is falsifiable — iff some possible observation would contradict it. Popper contrasted Einstein's general relativity, which risked refutation in the 1919 eclipse observations, with Freudian psychoanalysis and Marxist history as practised by their followers, which he thought could accommodate any outcome.

The Duhem–Quine problem

Pierre Duhem (The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory, 1906) observed that hypotheses never face experiment alone: deriving a prediction needs auxiliary assumptions a (about instruments, initial conditions, other theories). The real form is (h ∧ a) → o, and a failed prediction refutes only the conjunction:

Logic does not say which of h and a to reject. When Uranus's orbit deviated from Newtonian predictions, astronomers rejected an auxiliary (that there are no further planets) and Neptune was discovered (1846). When Mercury's orbit deviated, a similar move (the planet “Vulcan”) failed, and the anomaly was eventually explained by relativity. Quine extended the point (the Duhem–Quine thesis): any statement can be held true come what may, by adjusting enough elsewhere. Popper replied that ad hoc rescues — adjustments with no independent testable consequences — are methodologically forbidden; Imre Lakatos (“Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes”, 1970) developed this into a theory of progressive and degenerating research programmes.

The paradox of the ravens

Carl Hempel (“Studies in the Logic of Confirmation”, 1945) found a puzzle for the idea that instances confirm generalisations. A black raven confirms “All ravens are black”, ∀x(Rx → Bx). This is logically equivalent to “All non-black things are non-ravens”, ∀x(¬Bx → ¬Rx), which is confirmed by a white shoe. If equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by the same evidence, a white shoe confirms that all ravens are black — the paradox of the ravens. The equivalence is a matter of first-order logic:

Hempel himself accepted the conclusion and explained away the intuition; Bayesians argue that a white shoe does confirm the hypothesis, but to a minuscule degree, because non-black things vastly outnumber ravens.

Kuhn and scientific revolutions

Thomas Kuhn (The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, 1962) argued that Popper's picture does not fit the history of science. Most science is normal science, conducted within a paradigm — a shared framework of theory, exemplary problem solutions, methods and standards (Newtonian mechanics; Ptolemaic astronomy). Normal scientists do not try to refute the paradigm; they solve puzzles within it, and treat failures as failures of the scientist, not of the theory. Anomalies accumulate; eventually a crisis leads to a revolution, in which a new paradigm replaces the old (Copernicus, Lavoisier, Einstein). Kuhn claimed that rival paradigms are incommensurable: they differ in their concepts, problems and standards, so that there is no neutral algorithm for choosing between them — choice involves values such as accuracy, simplicity and fruitfulness, weighed differently by different scientists. Critics charged him with making science irrational; Kuhn rejected that reading, insisting that theory choice is rational without being algorithmic.

Underdetermination and scientific realism

Underdetermination: for any body of evidence, there are (perhaps) incompatible theories that fit it equally well. In the strong form, there are empirically equivalent rivals that agree on all possible observations. If so, how can evidence give us reason to believe one theory rather than another?

Scientific realism holds that our best theories are approximately true, including what they say about unobservable entities like electrons and genes. The main argument is the no-miracles argument: Putnam (1975) said realism “is the only philosophy that doesn't make the success of science a miracle”. It is an inference to the best explanation: the best explanation of the success of our theories is that they are approximately true.

Anti-realist responses:

  • The pessimistic meta-induction (Larry Laudan, “A Confutation of Convergent Realism”, 1981): the history of science contains many successful theories later rejected as false — the caloric theory of heat, the ether theories of light. By induction, our current theories are probably false too.
  • Constructive empiricism (Bas van Fraassen, The Scientific Image, 1980): science aims at empirical adequacy — truth about observables — not truth. Accepting a theory involves believing only what it says about observable things. The success of theories is explained Darwinistically: unsuccessful theories are eliminated.
  • Structural realism (John Worrall, 1989): what survives theory change is mathematical structure, so we should be realists only about structure.

Part B: Philosophy of religion

Classical theism holds that there is a God who is omnipotent, omniscient and wholly good, who created the universe. The traditional arguments for God's existence come in two kinds: a priori (the ontological argument) and a posteriori (the cosmological and design arguments).

The ontological argument

Anselm of Canterbury (Proslogion, ch. 2, 1077–78) defined God as “that than which nothing greater can be conceived”. Even the fool who says in his heart that there is no God understands this phrase, so God exists in the understanding. Suppose God existed only in the understanding and not in reality. Then something greater could be conceived — namely, that being existing in reality. But nothing greater than God can be conceived. So God exists in reality. The argument is a reductio. Taking the domain to be conceivable beings, with Rx: x exists in reality, Gxy: x is greater than y, and g: that than which nothing greater can be conceived:

The argument is valid, so the debate is over its premises and over the legitimacy of the formalisation:

  • The domain includes merely conceivable beings, some of which do not exist in reality, and “exists in reality” is treated as a predicate that some of them lack. Kant (Critique of Pure Reason, 1781) objected that “being is obviously not a real predicate”: to say that something exists adds nothing to the concept of it, so existence cannot make a being “greater”. Frege and Russell later held that existence is expressed by the quantifier, not by a predicate, which supports Kant's diagnosis.
  • Gaunilo, a contemporary monk, offered a parody: an island than which no greater island can be conceived must exist, by the same reasoning. Since that is absurd, something is wrong with the reasoning. Anselm replied that the argument works only for a being whose greatness is unlimited.
  • Is the name g legitimate? First-order logic assumes every name refers to something in the domain. If the domain is real things, assuming a referent for g begs the question; if it includes merely conceivable things, we need a theory of such objects.

Alvin Plantinga (The Nature of Necessity, 1974) gave a modal version: if it is possible that a maximally great being (one that exists necessarily with maximal excellence) exists, then, in the modal logic S5 (Chapter 9), it follows that such a being exists. The inference is valid in S5 (since ◇□p → □p is an S5 theorem), so everything depends on the premise that a maximally great being is possible — and critics note that, by parallel reasoning, if it is possible that no such being exists, then none does.

The cosmological argument

Cosmological arguments reason from the existence of the universe, or of change or causation in it, to a first cause or necessary being. Aquinas's first three “Five Ways” (Summa Theologiae I, q. 2, a. 3) are cosmological. The kalām argument, from medieval Islamic philosophy (al-Ghazālī) and revived by William Lane Craig (The Kalām Cosmological Argument, 1979), runs:

  1. P1Whatever begins to exist has a cause.
  2. P2The universe began to exist.
  3. CThe universe has a cause.

With Bx: x began to exist, Cyx: y causes x, and a: the universe:

The argument is valid. Defenders support P2 with philosophical arguments against an actually infinite past and with Big Bang cosmology; critics question whether causal principles drawn from within the universe apply to the universe as a whole, and whether the beginning of time leaves room for a cause “before” it. Note too how far the conclusion is from theism: a further argument is needed to show that the cause is a single, personal, omnipotent and good being.

The quantifier shift fallacy

Some popular versions of the argument from causation run: “everything has a cause; so there is something that is the cause of everything”. This is invalid. It moves from ∀x∃y Cyx to ∃y∀x Cyx — the quantifier shift fallacy (compare “everyone has a mother; so someone is everyone's mother”). A two-object counter-model, in which each thing causes the other, makes the premise true and the conclusion false:

Interpretation
  • Domain: {1, 2}
  • Extension of C: {⟨1,2⟩, ⟨2,1⟩}
In this interpretation
  • ∀x∃yCyx is true
  • ∃y∀xCyx is false

(The converse inference, from ∃y∀x to ∀x∃y, is valid.) Aquinas's own arguments are more careful: they argue that certain causal series cannot regress infinitely, and so must have a first member — a substantive premise, not a quantifier slip.

Moving from ∀x∃y to ∃y∀x is invalid. The third option (∃y∀x to ∀x∃y) is valid, and the first is just ∀E.

The design argument

William Paley (Natural Theology, 1802) argued by analogy: if you found a watch on a heath, its intricate arrangement of parts for a purpose would lead you to infer a designer; living things show far more intricate adaptation of means to ends; so they too have a designer. Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (published 1779, before Paley wrote) had already raised the main objections:

  • The analogy between the universe and human artefacts is weak; we have experience of watches being made, but of only one universe, and none of universes being made.
  • Even if sound, the argument would not establish a single, infinite, perfect designer: perhaps a committee of gods, or an infant deity's first botched attempt.
  • Order might arise from other causes — a point Darwin's theory of natural selection (On the Origin of Species, 1859) later made precise for biological adaptation.

Modern versions understand the argument not as an analogy but as an inference to the best explanation, often based on the fine-tuning of physical constants for life (Richard Swinburne, The Existence of God, 1979). Critics invoke the anthropic principle (we could only observe a universe compatible with observers) and the possibility of a multiverse. Since such arguments are inductive or probabilistic, they cannot be checked for deductive validity; their assessment belongs to the theory of confirmation.

The problem of evil

The logical problem

J. L. Mackie (“Evil and Omnipotence”, 1955) argued that theism is not merely unsupported but inconsistent. The theist holds: God is omnipotent (o), omniscient (k) and wholly good (w); and evil exists (e). So the set is inconsistent. The theist must reject a premise — and the only candidate that does not abandon theism is premise 2: an omnipotent, omniscient, good being would prevent all evil. The free will defence

Alvin Plantinga (God, Freedom, and Evil, 1974) argued that premise 2 is not a necessary truth. Distinguish a theodicy, which claims to give God's actual reasons, from a defence, which only needs to describe a possible state of affairs in which God and evil coexist, to show the set consistent. The free will defence: a world containing free creatures who freely do good is more valuable than one without free creatures; God cannot cause creatures to freely do only good (that is a contradiction, and omnipotence does not include the power to do the logically impossible); and it is possible that every free creature God could create would go wrong at least once (“transworld depravity”). If this is possible, God and evil are consistent, and the logical problem is answered. Mackie had anticipated a version, asking why God could not have made creatures who always freely choose the good; Plantinga's reply is that whether they would do so is not up to God.

Natural evil (earthquakes, disease) is harder; Plantinga suggests it is logically possible that it is caused by free non-human agents, which is enough for consistency, though few find it plausible as a real explanation. John Hick (Evil and the God of Love, 1966) offered a “soul-making” theodicy: a world with challenges and suffering is needed for moral and spiritual growth.

Many philosophers now consider the logical problem answered, and focus on the evidential problem of evil (William Rowe, “The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism”, 1979): an omnipotent, wholly good being would prevent intense suffering unless it could not do so without losing some greater good or permitting something worse; there probably exist instances of such suffering — Rowe's example is a fawn trapped in a forest fire, dying slowly and unobserved — that serve no such purpose; so there probably is no such being. Here the dispute is about probability rather than consistency. “Sceptical theists” reply that we are in no position to judge that apparently pointless suffering is really pointless.

Summary

  • Confirmation by successful prediction is not deductively valid (affirming the consequent); falsification is (modus tollens). Popper built his philosophy of science on this asymmetry.
  • The Duhem–Quine thesis: a failed prediction refutes only the conjunction of hypothesis and auxiliaries.
  • Hempel's ravens paradox arises because ∀x(Rx → Bx) and ∀x(¬Bx → ¬Rx) are equivalent.
  • Kuhn described normal science within paradigms, and revolutions between incommensurable paradigms.
  • Realists argue from the success of science (no miracles); anti-realists cite underdetermination and the pessimistic meta-induction; van Fraassen defends constructive empiricism.
  • Anselm's ontological argument is a valid reductio whose formalisation treats existence as a predicate, which Kant rejected.
  • The kalām argument is valid; “everything has a cause, so something causes everything” is a quantifier shift fallacy.
  • The design argument is inductive; Hume and Darwin undercut its analogical form.
  • Mackie's logical problem of evil needs a premise linking goodness and omnipotence to the absence of evil; Plantinga's free will defence argues that this premise is not necessary, so theism is consistent. The evidential problem remains.

Key terms

Falsificationism
Popper's view that scientific theories are distinguished by being falsifiable and are tested by attempts to refute them.
Duhem–Quine thesis
The thesis that hypotheses are tested only together with auxiliary assumptions, so a failed prediction does not by itself show which assumption is false.
Paradox of the ravens
Hempel's puzzle: if instances confirm generalisations and equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by the same evidence, a white shoe confirms "all ravens are black".
Paradigm
Kuhn's term for the framework of theories, methods and exemplars shared by a scientific community.
Underdetermination
The thesis that evidence does not uniquely determine which theory is true.
Scientific realism
The view that mature scientific theories are approximately true, including about unobservables.
No-miracles argument
Putnam's argument that scientific realism is the only view that does not make the success of science a miracle.
Inference to the best explanation
See abduction.
Pessimistic meta-induction
Laudan's argument that, since many successful past theories were false, current successful theories are probably false too.
Constructive empiricism
Van Fraassen's view that science aims only at empirically adequate theories, and accepting a theory involves believing only what it says about observables.
Theism
Belief in a God who is omnipotent, omniscient and wholly good.
Ontological argument
An a priori argument that God exists, from the concept of God (Anselm, Descartes, Plantinga).
Cosmological argument
An argument from the existence of the universe, or of contingent things, to a first cause or necessary being.
Quantifier shift fallacy
The invalid inference from "everything has something that Rs it" (∀x∃y) to "something Rs everything" (∃y∀x).
Design argument
An argument from apparent design or fine-tuning in nature to a designer.
Problem of evil
The challenge that the existence of evil is incompatible with, or makes unlikely, the existence of God.
Free will defence
Plantinga's argument that God and evil are logically consistent, because God may have had good reason to create free creatures who could do wrong.
Evidential problem of evil
The argument that the amount and kinds of suffering make God's existence improbable, even if they are logically consistent with it.

Exercise 15.2

Is Freudian theory unfalsifiable? Explain Popper's claim and give one reason to doubt it.

Show solution

Popper claimed that psychoanalysis could explain any behaviour — e.g. both a man who pushes a child into water and one who saves it — so no observation could count against it. Critics (e.g. Adolf Grünbaum) argued that Freudian theory does make testable claims (e.g. about the therapeutic efficacy of insight), some of which have been tested and arguably refuted — so it is falsifiable, though perhaps false.

Exercise 15.5

Explain the difference between a defence and a theodicy, and why Plantinga needs only a defence against Mackie.

Show solution

A theodicy claims to state God's actual reasons for permitting evil; a defence only describes a logically possible scenario in which God and evil coexist. Mackie claimed that theism is inconsistent; to rebut a charge of inconsistency it suffices to show that the beliefs could all be true together — i.e. that a model exists — and a possible scenario provides one. Whether the scenario is actual or probable is irrelevant to consistency.
