Chapter 1: What analytic philosophy is

Exercise 1.1

In one or two sentences each, say what the following contributed to analytic philosophy: (a) Frege's Begriffsschrift; (b) Russell's “On Denoting”; (c) Quine's “Two Dogmas of Empiricism”; (d) Kripke's Naming and Necessity.

Show answer

(a) It introduced modern quantificational logic, with variables and quantifiers, able to represent multiple generality and the structure of mathematical proof. (b) It analysed definite descriptions (“the F”) as quantified claims, showing that grammatical form can differ from logical form and solving puzzles about non-referring terms. (c) It attacked the analytic–synthetic distinction and the idea that statements are confirmed individually, defending holism and paving the way for naturalism. (d) It argued that names are rigid designators whose reference is fixed causally rather than by descriptions, and that some necessary truths are a posteriori, reviving metaphysics.

Exercise 1.2

The verification principle says that a statement is meaningful only if it is analytic or empirically verifiable. Explain the objection that the principle is self-refuting. Can you think of a reply a positivist might give?

Show answer

The principle itself seems neither analytic (it is not true by definition of “meaningful”, since many competent speakers deny it) nor empirically verifiable (no observation could confirm it). So by its own standard it is meaningless. A positivist might reply that the principle is not a statement of fact but a proposal or recommendation about how to use the word “meaningful” — to be judged by its usefulness for clarifying science, not by whether it is true. (Ayer and Carnap both took roughly this line.) A critic can then ask why we should accept the proposal, and whether its usefulness is enough to support the dismissal of metaphysics.

Exercise 1.3

Identify the equivocation in this argument: “The end of a thing is its perfection. Death is the end of life. So death is the perfection of life.”

Show answer

“End” means goal or purpose in the first premise and termination in the second. With one meaning held fixed, one of the premises becomes false (or the argument becomes invalid). The argument only seems persuasive because the word shifts meaning.

Exercise 1.4

Choose a short passage from any philosophical text you are studying. (a) State its thesis in one sentence. (b) List the reasons given for it. (c) Identify the claim you find least convincing and say why.

Show answer

Answers depend on the passage chosen. A good answer states the thesis in the author's terms but in your own words; lists the reasons as separate numbered premises (adding any unstated ones the argument needs); and, in (c), targets a specific premise or inferential step, explaining why it is doubtful — ideally with a counterexample — rather than simply disagreeing with the conclusion. Compare your reconstruction with a classmate's: if you identified different theses, reread the passage.

Exercise 1.5

“Analytic philosophy is clear; continental philosophy is obscure.” Write a paragraph assessing this claim fairly.

Show answer

A good answer notes that the claim is a caricature. Analytic writing does tend to value plain statement and explicit argument, while some continental writing deliberately uses literary or difficult styles, sometimes for principled reasons (for instance, the view that the form of writing cannot be separated from its content). But clarity varies within both traditions: some analytic work is technical to the point of inaccessibility, and many continental philosophers (e.g. Sartre in his essays, or Habermas) argue explicitly. The traditions also overlap in topics. A fair conclusion is that the distinction marks tendencies of style and method rather than a difference in intellectual virtue.

Chapter 2: Arguments

Exercise 2.1

Identify the premises and conclusion: “Since the mind can doubt the existence of the body but cannot doubt its own existence, the mind is not the same thing as the body.”

Show answer

P1: I can doubt that my body exists. P2: I cannot doubt that my mind exists. C: My mind is not the same thing as my body. (This is a version of an argument associated with Descartes. To be valid it needs a premise like “If x and y are identical, whatever is true of x is true of y” — Leibniz's Law — and it is controversial whether “being doubted by me” is the kind of property to which that law applies. See Chapter 13.)

Exercise 2.2

True or false? Explain each answer. (a) Every sound argument is valid. (b) Every valid argument is sound. (c) A valid argument can have a false conclusion. (d) If an argument has a false conclusion, at least one premise is false. (e) An invalid argument can have true premises and a true conclusion.

Show answer

(a) True: soundness is defined as validity plus true premises. (b) False: a valid argument may have false premises. (c) True, provided at least one premise is false. (d) False in general; it holds only for valid arguments. An invalid argument can have true premises and a false conclusion. (e) True — e.g. “Paris is in France; so water is H₂O.”

Exercise 2.3

Use the counterexample method to show that denying the antecedent (If A then B; not A; so not B) is invalid.

Show answer

“If Rex is a cat, Rex is a mammal. Rex is not a cat. So Rex is not a mammal.” If Rex is a dog, both premises are true and the conclusion false. So the form is invalid.

Exercise 2.4

Reconstruct in standard form, supplying any missing premise: “You shouldn't trust that newspaper — it's owned by a billionaire.”

Show answer

P1: The newspaper is owned by a billionaire. P2 (unstated): No newspaper owned by a billionaire should be trusted. C: You should not trust the newspaper. P2 is very strong and probably false as stated; a more charitable version might be “Newspapers owned by billionaires have an incentive to slant coverage in their owners' interests, so their reporting on those interests should be checked against other sources” — which supports a much weaker conclusion.

Exercise 2.5

Name the fallacy (if any) in each: (a) “Nobody has ever shown that ghosts don't exist, so they do.” (b) “Each player is excellent, so the team must be excellent.” (c) “Either you support this law or you don't care about children.” (d) “After I started wearing my lucky socks we won three matches, so the socks work.”

Show answer

(a) Appeal to ignorance. (b) Composition — excellent individuals may play badly together. (c) False dilemma. (d) Post hoc ergo propter hoc (and hasty generalisation from three cases).

Exercise 2.6

Explain the difference between an argument and an explanation, using an example of each that contains the word “because”.

Show answer

Argument: “The suspect cannot have done it, because she was in Glasgow at the time.” The speaker is trying to convince us that the suspect did not do it, giving a reason. Explanation: “The ice melted because the temperature rose above 0 °C.” Here the melting is taken for granted and the speaker says what caused it. Test: is the “conclusion” already accepted (explanation) or in dispute (argument)?

Chapter 3: Propositional logic: language and translation

Exercise 3.1

Which of the following are well-formed formulas (allowing the conventions about outer brackets and ¬)? (a) ¬(p ∨ q) (b) (p ∧) q (c) p → (q → (r → s)) (d) ¬¬¬p ∨ ¬q (e) (p ↔ q)) (f) p ∨ q ∨

Show answer

(a) yes; (b) no; (c) yes; (d) yes — main connective ∨; (e) no (unbalanced brackets); (f) no.

Exercise 3.2

Give the main connective of each formula: (a) ¬p ∧ q (b) ¬(p ∧ q) (c) (p → q) ↔ (¬q → ¬p) (d) p → (q ∨ r) (e) ¬(p → q) → ¬r

Show answer

(a) ∧; (b) ¬; (c) ↔; (d) →; (e) the second → (the one outside the brackets).

Exercise 3.3

Using the key s: Sam sings; d: Dee dances; m: the music plays, translate: (a) Sam sings only if the music plays. (b) Dee dances if the music plays. (c) Neither Sam sings nor Dee dances. (d) Sam sings unless Dee dances. (e) The music playing is necessary and sufficient for Dee dancing. (f) If the music plays, Sam sings or Dee dances, but not both.

Show answer

(a) s → m; (b) m → d; (c) ¬s ∧ ¬d; (d) s ∨ d; (e) m ↔ d; (f) m → ((s ∨ d) ∧ ¬(s ∧ d)).

Exercise 3.4

Translate into natural English, using the key of Exercise 3.3: (a) ¬(s ∧ d) (b) ¬m → (¬s ∧ ¬d) (c) d → (m ∧ s)

Show answer

(a) Sam doesn't sing and Dee dance together (they don't both perform). (b) If the music doesn't play, neither Sam sings nor Dee dances. (c) Dee dances only if the music plays and Sam sings.

Exercise 3.5

Explain why “You will pass only if you revise” does not mean the same as “If you revise, you will pass”. Give a situation in which one is true and the other false.

Show answer

With p: you pass and r: you revise, the first is p → r (revising is necessary for passing); the second is r → p (revising is sufficient). Situation: you revise but fail. Then “if you revise you will pass” is false, but “you will pass only if you revise” is true (you didn't pass without revising — indeed you didn't pass at all).

Exercise 3.6

Translate, giving your key: “If Parliament passes the bill, the law changes, unless the courts strike it down; and the courts will strike it down only if it is unconstitutional.”

Show answer

Key: b: Parliament passes the bill; l: the law changes; c: the courts strike it down; u: it is unconstitutional. Translation: (b → (l ∨ c)) ∧ (c → u). (The first conjunct could also be read as (b → l) ∨ c; the reading on which “unless” attaches to the consequent seems more natural, but the ambiguity is worth noting. In fact the two readings are logically equivalent here.)

Chapter 4: Truth tables

Exercise 4.1

Let p = T, q = F, r = F. Compute: (a) p ∧ (q ∨ ¬r) (b) (p → q) → r (c) ¬(p ↔ q) ∧ ¬r (d) (q → r) ↔ ¬p

Exercise 4.3

Use truth tables to decide which pairs are equivalent: (a) p → (q ∨ r) and (p → q) ∨ (p → r); (b) (p ∨ q) → r and (p → r) ∨ (q → r); (c) ¬(p ↔ q) and ¬p ↔ q.

Show answer

(a) Equivalent. (b) Not equivalent: with p = T, q = F, r = F, the first is F but the second is T (since q → r is T). The correct equivalent of (p ∨ q) → r is the conjunction (p → r) ∧ (q → r). (c) Equivalent.

Exercise 4.5

Use the short truth-table method to test: (a) p → (q ∧ r), ¬r ∴ ¬p (b) p → q, r → q ∴ p → r (c) (p ∨ q) → r, ¬r ∴ ¬p ∧ ¬q

Show answer

(a) Conclusion F: p = T. Premise 2 T: r = F. Premise 1 T with p = T: q ∧ r = T, so r = T. Contradiction: valid.

(b) Conclusion F: p = T, r = F. Premise 1 T: q = T. Premise 2: r → q = F → T = T. Consistent: invalid, counterexample p = T, q = T, r = F.

(c) Conclusion F: ¬p ∧ ¬q F, so at least one of p, q is T — not forced, so look at the premises first. Premise 2: r = F. Premise 1 T with r = F: p ∨ q = F, so p = F and q = F. Then ¬p ∧ ¬q = T, contradicting our assumption. Valid.

Exercise 4.6

Is the set {p ∨ q, ¬p ∨ ¬q, p → q} consistent? If so, give a row that makes all members true.

Show answer

Consistent: p = F, q = T makes all three true (p ∨ q = T, ¬p ∨ ¬q = T, p → q = T).

Exercise 4.7

Explain, using truth tables, why A ⊨ B holds exactly when A → B is a tautology.

Show answer

A → B is false in a row exactly when A is true and B false in that row. So A → B is a tautology (false in no row) exactly when there is no row with A true and B false — which is the definition of A ⊨ B.

Exercise 4.8

“If I am in Edinburgh, I am in Scotland. So either if I am in Edinburgh I am in France, or if I am in France I am in Scotland.” Show that this is valid in propositional logic. Does this show the material conditional is a poor model of “if”? Discuss briefly.

Show answer

Formalise as e → s ∴ (e → f) ∨ (f → s). In fact the conclusion is a tautology by itself, so the argument is valid whatever the premise says. Short method: if the conclusion were F, both disjuncts would be F; e → f F requires f = F, but f → s F requires f = T — contradiction. The conclusion sounds absurd in English, suggesting that English “if” is not always material. A Gricean might reply that the disjunction is true but unassertable: nobody would assert a disjunction of conditionals when they are in a position to assert something stronger (here, that I am not in France, which makes f → s true).

Chapter 6: Predicate logic

Exercise 6.1

Translate, using Hx: x is a human, Mx: x is mortal, Gx: x is a god, z: Zeus. (a) No god is mortal. (b) Only humans are mortal. (c) Some humans are not mortal. (d) If Zeus is a god, then not every god is mortal. (e) Not all gods are immortal.

Show answer

(a) ∀x(Gx → ¬Mx) or ¬∃x(Gx ∧ Mx). (b) ∀x(Mx → Hx). (c) ∃x(Hx ∧ ¬Mx). (d) Gz → ¬∀x(Gx → Mx). (e) ¬∀x(Gx → ¬Mx), equivalently ∃x(Gx ∧ Mx).

Exercise 6.2

Domain: people. Lxy: x likes y; a: Ann. Translate: (a) Ann likes everyone. (b) Everyone likes Ann. (c) Someone likes everyone. (d) Everyone likes someone who likes them. (e) Nobody likes everyone. (f) Ann likes everyone who likes her.

Show answer

(a) ∀xLax. (b) ∀xLxa. (c) ∃x∀yLxy. (d) ∀x∃y(Lxy ∧ Lyx). (e) ¬∃x∀yLxy (equivalently ∀x∃y¬Lxy). (f) ∀x(Lxa → Lax).

Exercise 6.6

Using Russell's theory, give both readings of “The greatest prime number is not odd” and say which is true. (There is no greatest prime: Euclid proved there are infinitely many.)

Show answer

With Gx: x is a greatest prime, Ox: x is odd. Narrow-scope negation: ∃x(Gx ∧ ∀y(Gy → y = x) ∧ ¬Ox) — false, since nothing satisfies G. Wide-scope negation: ¬∃x(Gx ∧ ∀y(Gy → y = x) ∧ Ox) — true, for the same reason.

Chapter 8: A taste of metalogic

Exercise 8.1

Explain the difference between ⊢ and ⊨. Which theorem says that ⊢ implies ⊨, and which the converse?

Show answer

Γ ⊢ A: A is derivable from Γ by the rules (a syntactic notion). Γ ⊨ A: every interpretation making Γ true makes A true (a semantic notion). Soundness: ⊢ implies ⊨. Completeness: ⊨ implies ⊢.

Exercise 8.2

A student claims to have proved ∀x∃yRxy ⊢ ∃y∀xRxy in our system. Without looking at the proof, how do you know it contains a mistake?

Show answer

The argument is invalid: there is a counter-model (Chapter 6). By soundness, no invalid argument is provable. So the “proof” must break a rule — typically the restriction on ∀I.

Exercise 8.3

“Since first-order logic is undecidable, there are valid arguments we can never prove.” Is this right?

Show answer

No. By completeness, every valid argument has a proof. Undecidability means there is no single mechanical procedure that, for every argument, correctly answers “valid” or “invalid” in finite time. A proof-search procedure will eventually find a proof of any valid argument, but on invalid arguments it may run forever, and we cannot in general tell in advance which case we are in.

Exercise 8.4

Why does completeness of first-order logic not contradict Gödel's first incompleteness theorem?

Show answer

They concern different things. Completeness says every sentence true in all interpretations is provable. The incompleteness theorem concerns a particular theory of arithmetic: the Gödel sentence G is true in the intended interpretation (the natural numbers), but not in every model of the theory — there are non-standard models in which it is false. So G is not a logical consequence of the axioms, and its unprovability is exactly what completeness predicts.

Exercise 8.5

Explain informally why truth tables cannot serve as a decision procedure for predicate logic.

Show answer

A truth table checks finitely many rows. In predicate logic the analogue of a row is an interpretation, and there are infinitely many interpretations, with domains of every size including infinite ones; some arguments are invalid only because of an infinite counter-model. No finite table can survey them. (Church and Turing showed that no other mechanical procedure can do the job either.)

Chapter 9: Beyond classical logic

Exercise 9.1

Translate, using p: it rains: (a) It might rain. (b) It is necessary that it does not rain. (c) It is not necessary that it rains. (d) It is possible that it does not rain. Which pairs are equivalent?

Show answer

(a) ◇p. (b) □¬p. (c) ¬□p. (d) ◇¬p. (c) and (d) are equivalent; (a) and (b) are contradictories (each is equivalent to the negation of the other).

Exercise 9.2

In the model W = {w1, w2}, R = {⟨w1, w2⟩, ⟨w2, w2⟩}, with p true only at w2: what are the truth values at w1 of (a) p, (b) □p, (c) □□p, (d) □p → p? What does this show about axiom T?

Show answer

(a) F. (b) T (the only world accessible from w1 is w2, where p is true). (c) T (□p is true at w2, the only world w1 sees). (d) F. So T fails in this model; the relation is not reflexive (w1 does not see itself).

Exercise 9.3

Give an everyday example showing that “strengthening the antecedent” fails for counterfactuals.

Show answer

“If I had dropped the glass, it would have broken” may be true; “If I had dropped the glass and it had landed on a cushion, it would have broken” is false. The closest worlds where I drop the glass are not ones where a cushion happens to be beneath it.

Exercise 9.4

Show that ¬¬(p ∨ ¬p) can be proved without IP, DNE or TND (so it is intuitionistically valid).

Show answer

Only ∨I, ¬E and ¬I are used. Compare the classical proof of p ∨ ¬p in Chapter 5, which ends with IP instead of ¬I.

Exercise 9.5

In LP, show that disjunctive syllogism (p ∨ q, ¬p ⊨ q) fails.

Show answer

Let p = B and q = F. Then p ∨ q = B (designated) and ¬p = B (designated), but q = F (not designated). So the premises are at least true and the conclusion is not.

Exercise 9.6

Briefly assess Quine's slogan “change of logic, change of subject”.

Show answer

A good answer explains the slogan (a deviant logician who rejects, say, excluded middle must mean something different by “not” or “or”, so is not really contradicting the classical logician) and evaluates it: if correct, logical disagreement would be merely verbal, yet intuitionists and classical logicians seem to disagree about something substantive — e.g. whether certain mathematical proofs establish their conclusions. One might also note that the connectives' meanings could be fixed by shared core rules (such as ∧I/∧E) while disagreement concerns contested ones, or that Quine's own holism (any belief, even logic, is revisable) sits uneasily with the slogan.

Chapter 10: Language and meaning

Exercise 10.1

Explain Frege's puzzle and his solution. Then explain why “Lois Lane believes that Superman can fly” and “Lois Lane believes that Clark Kent can fly” can differ in truth value.

Show answer

“Superman = Clark Kent” is informative while “Superman = Superman” is trivial, though both concern one object. Frege: the names share a referent but differ in sense (mode of presentation). In belief reports, what matters is how Lois thinks of the object: she grasps the thought expressed by “Superman can fly” and accepts it, but not the different thought expressed by “Clark Kent can fly”. On Frege's view, in the belief context the names refer to their senses, which differ, so substitution need not preserve truth.

Exercise 10.2

Give Russellian analyses of (a) “The author of Middlemarch was a woman”, and (b) both readings of “The author of Middlemarch was not a man.” (Key: Ax: x wrote Middlemarch; Wx: x was a woman; Mx: x was a man.)

Show answer

(a) ∃x(Ax ∧ ∀y(Ay → y = x) ∧ Wx). (b) Narrow-scope negation: ∃x(Ax ∧ ∀y(Ay → y = x) ∧ ¬Mx); wide-scope: ¬∃x(Ax ∧ ∀y(Ay → y = x) ∧ Mx). Since Middlemarch has a unique author (George Eliot, i.e. Mary Ann Evans), both readings are true here; they would come apart if there were no unique author.

Set out Kripke's modal argument against descriptivism in premise–conclusion form. Which premise would a descriptivist most plausibly reject, and how?

Show answer

See the reconstruction in the chapter (a modus tollens). A descriptivist might reject P1 by holding that the description associated with the name takes wide scope over modal operators, or is rigidified by “actually” (“the actual teacher of Alexander”), so that “Aristotle taught Alexander” is not necessary even if the name abbreviates that description. Exercise 10.4

Identify the implicature and the maxim involved: (a) A: “Is there petrol nearby?” B: “There's a garage round the corner.” (b) “I ate some of the biscuits.” (c) A: “How was the concert?” B: “The hall was very nicely decorated.”

Show answer

(a) The garage is open and sells petrol (Relation). (b) I did not eat all of them (Quantity). (c) The concert was not good (Quantity/Relation, flouted: B conspicuously avoids the question). Each can be cancelled (“… but it's closed”, “… in fact all of them”, “… and the music was superb too”), confirming they are implicatures rather than what was said.

Exercise 10.5

Is “Water is H₂O” necessary? Is it a priori? Explain Kripke's view and one objection.

Show answer

Kripke: “water” is a rigid designator for a natural kind whose essence is its molecular structure, so in every world water is H₂O — necessary; but this was discovered empirically — a posteriori. Objection: perhaps “water” is associated with a description (“the clear, drinkable stuff in our rivers”) so that in worlds where XYZ plays that role, “water” would refer to XYZ; two-dimensional semantics (Chalmers, Jackson) tries to capture both intuitions by distinguishing two kinds of intension. Others question whether ordinary “water” really is H₂O, since what we call water contains impurities and the chemistry is more complex than the slogan suggests.

Exercise 10.6

“Quine's gavagai argument shows that we can never really understand each other.” Discuss.

Show answer

A good answer explains the argument and notes that Quine's conclusion is not that communication fails but that there is no fact of the matter about meaning beyond what behaviour fixes; communication proceeds smoothly because we share dispositions. It might discuss whether indeterminacy applies at home (Quine said yes), and objections: the argument presupposes behaviourism, and considerations of simplicity or naturalness (or innate cognitive structure, as Chomsky argued) may privilege one translation.

Chapter 11: Epistemology

Exercise 11.1

Construct your own Gettier case. Explain which conditions of the JTB analysis it satisfies and why it is not knowledge.

Show answer

Example: Mary sees what she takes to be her husband's car in the drive and believes he is home. The car is in fact a neighbour's identical car; but her husband is home, having arrived by taxi. Her belief is true, believed, and justified (seeing a familiar car is good evidence), but she does not know he is home, because what justifies her belief is disconnected from what makes it true.

Exercise 11.2

Explain how the sensitivity condition handles the fake-barn case, and why it leads Nozick to deny closure.

Show answer

Henry's belief “that's a barn” is insensitive: had it been a fake, he would still have believed it was a barn. So he doesn't know. Similarly my belief that I'm not a BIV is insensitive, so I don't know it; but my belief that I have hands is sensitive (in the nearest worlds where I lack hands — e.g. after an accident — I wouldn't believe I had them). So I know I have hands, I know that having hands entails not being a BIV, yet I don't know I'm not a BIV: closure fails.

Exercise 11.5

“Moore's proof begs the question.” Assess this claim.

Show answer

A good answer explains what begging the question is (assuming what is to be proved) and asks whether Moore assumes that the sceptic is wrong. Defenders note that Moore does not claim to persuade the sceptic, but to show that his own belief is more reasonable than any sceptical premise; the argument may be a perfectly good proof that fails to have dialectical force against someone who already doubts its premise (Crispin Wright and James Pryor have debated this under the heading of “transmission failure”).

Show answer

Exercise 12.5

Explain the pre-emption problem for the counterfactual theory of causation, using an example of your own.

Show answer

Example: two assassins; the first poisons the victim's drink, and the victim dies; had the first not done so, the backup assassin would have shot him. The first assassin caused the death, but the death does not counterfactually depend on the poisoning (he would have died anyway). So counterfactual dependence is not necessary for causation, and the simple theory needs refinement.

Chapter 13: Philosophy of mind

Exercise 13.1

Construct a masked man–style argument of your own that has the same form as Descartes' argument from doubt but obviously true premises and a false conclusion. What does this show?

Show answer

E.g. “Lois believes Superman can fly; Lois does not believe Clark Kent can fly; therefore Superman is not Clark Kent.” The premises are true (in the story) and the conclusion false, so the argument form — applying Leibniz's Law to belief or doubt contexts — is not valid in its natural-language reading. The formal proof is valid only because the symbolisation treats “believed by Lois to fly” as an ordinary property, which it is not.

Exercise 13.2

Explain the difference between type and token identity theory, and how the distinction helps with multiple realisability.

Show answer

Type identity: each mental type (pain) is identical to a physical type (C-fibre firing). Token identity: each particular mental event is identical to some particular physical event, but not necessarily one of a common physical type. Multiple realisability counts against type identity (octopus pain is not C-fibre firing) but is consistent with token identity (each octopus pain is some physical event).

It is valid (see the proof). The ability hypothesis says there is no fact Mary failed to know before release; what she lacked was know-how. So it rejects premise 2 (∃x¬Kx, read as “there is a fact Mary did not know”). It accepts that she gains something new, but denies that it is propositional knowledge of a fact.

Exercise 14.3

Explain why the open question argument resembles the masked man fallacy. Does this show that Moore was wrong?

Show answer

Both apply Leibniz's Law to properties that depend on how something is thought of (“being an open question whether…”, “being doubted by me”). Since “water = H₂O” was a substantive discovery while its openness was real, openness cannot show non-identity of properties. This shows the argument doesn't establish that goodness is not a natural property, though it may establish that “good” is not analytically definable in natural terms — a weaker but still interesting conclusion. Some defenders reply that Moore was interested in analysis (definition) anyway.

Exercise 14.4

Construct a valid moral argument using modus tollens and explain the Frege–Geach problem for it.

Show answer

E.g. “If eating meat is wrong, then factory farming is wrong. Factory farming is not wrong. So eating meat is not wrong.” Valid by MT. The expressivist must explain the meaning of “eating meat is wrong” in the antecedent (no disapproval expressed) and of “factory farming is not wrong” (what attitude does a negated moral sentence express — tolerance? not disapproving?), in a way that makes the argument valid rather than equivocal.

Exercise 15.2

Is Freudian theory unfalsifiable? Explain Popper's claim and give one reason to doubt it.

Show answer

Popper claimed that psychoanalysis could explain any behaviour — e.g. both a man who pushes a child into water and one who saves it — so no observation could count against it. Critics (e.g. Adolf Grünbaum) argued that Freudian theory does make testable claims (e.g. about the therapeutic efficacy of insight), some of which have been tested and arguably refuted — so it is falsifiable, though perhaps false.

Exercise 15.5

Explain the difference between a defence and a theodicy, and why Plantinga needs only a defence against Mackie.

Show answer

A theodicy claims to state God's actual reasons for permitting evil; a defence only describes a logically possible scenario in which God and evil coexist. Mackie claimed that theism is inconsistent; to rebut a charge of inconsistency it suffices to show that the beliefs could all be true together — i.e. that a model exists — and a possible scenario provides one. Whether the scenario is actual or probable is irrelevant to consistency.

In Newcomb's problem, at what accuracy of the Predictor do the expected values of one-boxing and two-boxing (calculated evidentially) become equal?

Show answer

Let the accuracy be q. One-box: q × 1,000,000. Two-box: (1 − q) × 1,001,000 + q × 1,000 = 1,001,000 − 1,000,000q. Setting equal: 1,000,000q = 1,001,000 − 1,000,000q, so q = 1,001,000 / 2,000,000 = 0.5005. Above about 50.05% accuracy, evidential expected value favours one-boxing.
