Philosophy of mind

Learning objectives

  • state substance dualism and Descartes' arguments for it, and recognise the masked man fallacy;
  • explain the interaction problem and Kim's exclusion argument, and formalise the latter;
  • describe behaviourism, the identity theory and functionalism, and the main objection to each;
  • formalise the knowledge argument and outline Nagel's bat and the zombie argument;
  • explain the Turing test and Searle's Chinese room;
  • state the hard problem of consciousness.

The philosophy of mind asks what minds are and how they relate to the physical world. You are thinking about this sentence; your brain is a system of about 86 billion neurons. How are these facts related? Are thoughts and experiences physical states of the brain, something over and above them, or something else entirely? This is the mind–body problem.

Substance dualism

Substance dualism holds that the mind is a non-physical thing (a soul or mental substance), distinct from the body. Its classic defender is Descartes (Meditations, 1641).

The argument from doubt — and a fallacy

In the Second Meditation, Descartes finds he can doubt that his body exists (perhaps a demon deceives him), but he cannot doubt that he exists as a thinking thing. One tempting reconstruction uses Leibniz's Law. Let Dx: I can doubt that x exists; m: my mind; b: my body.

The proof is formally impeccable. Yet the argument is unsound, or at least its first-order form misrepresents it. Compare: I can doubt that the masked man robbed the bank; I cannot doubt that my father robbed the bank (I watched him); therefore my father is not the masked man. Plainly this does not follow — my father might be the masked man without my knowing it. This is the masked man fallacy. Properties like “being doubted by me” are intensional (Chapter 10): they depend on how an object is thought of, not just on the object. Leibniz's Law applies to genuine properties of things; “can be doubted by me to exist” is not one. The lesson: a valid proof is only as good as the adequacy of its symbolisation.

The conceivability argument

In the Sixth Meditation Descartes gives a better argument: I can clearly and distinctly conceive of my mind existing without my body; whatever I can clearly and distinctly conceive is possible (God could bring it about); so it is possible for my mind to exist without my body; and if two things are identical, it is not possible for one to exist without the other (identity is necessary — compare Kripke in Chapter 10). Therefore mind and body are distinct. The weak point is the move from conceivability to possibility: one might conceive of water not being H₂O, though (if Kripke is right) that is impossible.

The interaction problem

Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, in her correspondence with Descartes (1643), asked how an immaterial soul with no extension could move a material body, since all causing of motion seems to involve contact. Descartes' answer — the union of mind and body is known through the senses and not understood by the intellect — did not satisfy her. Modern physics sharpens the problem through the principle of the causal closure of the physical: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause.

Some dualists accept epiphenomenalism: mental states are caused by physical states but cause nothing themselves. But then my pain does not cause my saying “ouch” — and, worse, my beliefs about my experiences are not caused by those experiences.

Behaviourism

Gilbert Ryle (The Concept of Mind, 1949) mocked Cartesian dualism as “the dogma of the Ghost in the Machine” and diagnosed it as a category mistake, like that of a visitor who, shown Oxford's colleges, libraries and laboratories, asks “but where is the University?” Mental terms, on Ryle's view, do not name inner episodes but dispositions to behave. To be brittle is to be disposed to shatter when struck; to be vain is to be disposed to talk about oneself, seek praise, and so on.

Logical behaviourism (Carl Hempel, partly Ryle) holds that every mental statement can be translated into statements about behaviour and behavioural dispositions. Objections:

  • Circularity: the behaviour associated with a belief depends on desires (someone who believes it is raining takes an umbrella only if they want to stay dry), and vice versa. Mental states can be defined behaviourally only in terms of other mental states.
  • Super-Spartans (Putnam, “Brains and Behavior”, 1963): imagine people who feel pain but have trained themselves never to show any sign of it. They have pain without pain behaviour, and (conversely) a skilled actor has pain behaviour without pain.

The identity theory

U. T. Place (“Is Consciousness a Brain Process?”, 1956) and J. J. C. Smart (“Sensations and Brain Processes”, 1959) proposed that mental states are brain states — not by definition, but as a matter of empirical discovery, as lightning turned out to be electrical discharge. Pain, for example, might be (the textbook placeholder) C-fibre firing. This identity theory solves the interaction problem at a stroke: mental causes are physical causes.

The main objection is multiple realisability (Putnam, “Psychological Predicates”, 1967). Octopuses, whose nervous systems are very different from ours, presumably feel pain; so might Martians or suitably built robots. (The type-identity claim is stronger than a biconditional, but entails it, so refuting the biconditional refutes the identity.) Identity theorists respond by restricting identities to species (“pain-in-humans is C-fibre firing”) or by distinguishing type identity from token identity: each particular pain is some physical event or other, even if pains have no single physical type in common.

Functionalism

Functionalism (Putnam, 1967; David Armstrong and David Lewis in a different form) defines mental states by their causal role: their typical causes, their effects on behaviour, and their relations to other mental states. Pain is whatever state is caused by bodily damage, causes distress and the desire to escape, and produces wincing and avoidance. Just as a mousetrap can be made of wood and springs or of plastic and glue, so pain can be realised in neurons, silicon, or octopus tissue. Functionalism keeps the behaviourist's insight that mental states are tied to behaviour, but treats them as real inner states (avoiding the circularity objection, since all the states are defined together), and it respects multiple realisability. Many see the mind as the software of the brain.

Objections focus on consciousness. Ned Block (“Troubles with Functionalism”, 1978) imagined the population of China organised, by radio links, to duplicate the functional organisation of a brain for an hour. Functionalism implies that the system has experiences; Block found that absurd. And inverted spectrum cases — someone whose experience of red is like my experience of green, though functionally identical to me — suggest that qualitative character is not fixed by functional role.

Quick check Which view best accommodates the possibility that an octopus and a human can both be in pain?

Functionalism defines pain by causal role, which can be realised in very different physical systems. This is the multiple realisability argument.

Consciousness

What is it like to be a bat?

Thomas Nagel (“What Is It Like to Be a Bat?”, 1974) argued that an organism has conscious mental states iff “there is something that it is like to be that organism”. Bats perceive by echolocation; we can know every physical fact about their sonar, but we cannot know what it is like for the bat. Physical science describes the world objectively, from no particular point of view; experience is essentially subjective, tied to a point of view. So, Nagel concluded, we have at present no conception of how physicalism could be true — though he did not claim it was false.

The felt qualities of experience — the redness of red, the painfulness of pain — are called qualia.

The knowledge argument

Frank Jackson (“Epiphenomenal Qualia”, 1982) described Mary, a brilliant scientist who has lived all her life in a black-and-white room, learning all the physical facts about colour vision from black-and-white books and television. When she is released and sees a red tomato, does she learn something new? Jackson says yes: she learns what it is like to see red. So not all facts are physical, and physicalism is false. This is the knowledge argument. In predicate logic, with the domain consisting of facts, Px: x is a physical fact, and Kx: Mary knew x before her release:

The argument is valid, so physicalists must deny a premise or argue that the symbolisation equivocates on “fact” or “knows”. Responses:

  • The ability hypothesis (Laurence Nemirow, Lewis): Mary gains know-how — abilities to recognise, imagine and remember red — not knowledge of a new fact.
  • The acquaintance hypothesis (Earl Conee): she becomes acquainted with a property she already knew about.
  • The phenomenal concepts strategy (Brian Loar): Mary acquires a new concept of an old, physical fact — a new mode of presentation of the same referent, as in Frege's Hesperus and Phosphorus.
  • Deny the first premise (Daniel Dennett): if Mary really knew all the physical facts, she would not be surprised; our intuitions fail for such an extreme case.

Jackson himself later became a physicalist (from the late 1990s), accepting a version of the reply that her new knowledge involves new representations.

Zombies and the hard problem

A philosophical zombie is a being physically identical to you, behaving exactly as you do, but with no conscious experience. David Chalmers (The Conscious Mind, 1996) argues: zombies are conceivable; what is conceivable (in an ideal sense) is possible; if zombies are possible, then the physical facts do not fix the facts about consciousness; so physicalism is false. As with Descartes, the debate centres on the conceivability–possibility link.

Chalmers (“Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness”, 1995) distinguishes the “easy problems” — explaining discrimination, attention, report and the control of behaviour, which are problems of explaining functions — from the hard problem of consciousness: why is the performance of these functions accompanied by experience at all? Even a complete account of the functions seems to leave this question open. (Joseph Levine called this the explanatory gap, 1983.)

Machines and minds

Alan Turing (“Computing Machinery and Intelligence”, 1950) proposed replacing the question “Can machines think?” with a test, the “imitation game”, now called the Turing test: an interrogator converses by text with a human and a machine and tries to tell which is which; a machine that does as well as the human passes.

John Searle (“Minds, Brains, and Programs”, 1980) argued that passing such a test would not be sufficient for understanding. In the Chinese room, Searle, who understands no Chinese, sits in a room with a rulebook (in English) for manipulating Chinese symbols. Questions in Chinese are passed in; by following the rules, he passes out appropriate answers. To outsiders, the room understands Chinese. But Searle understands nothing: he manipulates symbols by their shape alone. A computer running a program does just what Searle does. So running a program (syntax) is not sufficient for understanding (semantics). Searle's conclusion targets “strong AI” — the claim that an appropriately programmed computer literally has a mind.

Replies: the systems reply — Searle is only part of the system, and the whole system (man, rulebook, papers) understands. Searle answers that he could memorise the rules and do the calculations in his head, and still understand nothing. The robot reply — put the program in a robot that interacts with the world, grounding its symbols. Searle replies that adding sensors only adds more symbols. The debate has gained new urgency with large language models.

Mental causation and the exclusion argument

Non-reductive physicalists hold that mental properties are not identical to physical properties, but supervene on them: there can be no mental difference without a physical difference. Jaegwon Kim (Mind in a Physical World, 1998) argued that this position cannot allow mental causation, by the causal exclusion argument. So if mental properties are causally efficacious, physics is closed, and there is no systematic overdetermination, mental properties are not distinct from physical ones — reductive physicalism. Non-reductivists reject premise 1 (perhaps the mental and physical causes are not competitors, because the one realises the other), or argue that the argument “generalises” to biology and chemistry, which would be absurd.

For the most radical position, eliminative materialism (Paul Churchland, “Eliminative Materialism and the Propositional Attitudes”, 1981): folk psychology — the everyday theory of beliefs and desires — is a stagnant, false theory, likely to be replaced by neuroscience, just as talk of phlogiston was replaced by chemistry. Critics reply that the view is self-undermining: Churchland believes eliminativism.

Summary

  • Descartes argued for substance dualism; the argument from doubt commits the masked man fallacy, while the conceivability argument depends on the link from conceivability to possibility.
  • Dualism faces the interaction problem; epiphenomenalism avoids it at a heavy cost.
  • Behaviourism analyses mental states as behavioural dispositions, but faces circularity and super-Spartan objections.
  • The identity theory identifies mental with brain states; multiple realisability counts against type identity.
  • Functionalism defines mental states by causal role; the China brain and inverted spectrum challenge it.
  • Nagel's bat, Jackson's Mary and Chalmers's zombies argue that consciousness is not captured by physical facts — the hard problem.
  • Searle's Chinese room argues that syntax is not sufficient for semantics.
  • Kim's exclusion argument presses non-reductive physicalists on mental causation.

Key terms

Dualism
The view that the mind is not physical: either a distinct substance (substance dualism) or has non-physical properties (property dualism).
Masked man fallacy
Invalidly applying Leibniz's Law to intensional properties such as being believed or doubted by someone.
Epiphenomenalism
The view that mental states are caused by physical states but have no physical effects.
Behaviourism
In philosophy of mind, the view that mental states are dispositions to behave in certain ways.
Category mistake
Ryle's term for presenting facts of one logical type as if they belonged to another, e.g. looking for the university over and above its colleges.
Identity theory
The view that mental states are identical to brain states (type identity theory).
Multiple realisability
The thesis that one mental kind can be realised by many different physical kinds.
Functionalism
The view that mental states are defined by their causal roles — their relations to inputs, outputs and other mental states.
Qualia
The subjective, felt qualities of experience — "what it is like" to see red or feel pain.
Physicalism
The view that everything is physical, or supervenes on the physical.
Knowledge argument
Jackson's argument (Mary the colour scientist) that physical knowledge is not all knowledge.
Philosophical zombie
A hypothetical being physically identical to a conscious human but with no conscious experience at all.
Hard problem of consciousness
Chalmers's name for the problem of explaining why physical processes are accompanied by subjective experience at all.
Turing test
Turing's "imitation game": a machine passes if an interrogator, conversing by text, cannot reliably tell it from a human.
Chinese room
Searle's thought experiment intended to show that running a program is not sufficient for understanding.
Supervenience
A-properties supervene on B-properties if there can be no difference in A without a difference in B.
Mental causation
The causal influence of mental states on physical events, such as bodily movements.
Causal exclusion argument
Kim's argument that, given the causal closure of the physical and no systematic overdetermination, distinct mental properties cannot cause physical effects.
Eliminative materialism
The view that folk-psychological states such as beliefs and desires do not exist, and that folk psychology is a false theory to be replaced by neuroscience.

Exercises

Exercise 13.1

Construct a masked man–style argument of your own that has the same form as Descartes' argument from doubt but obviously true premises and a false conclusion. What does this show?

Show solution

E.g. “Lois believes Superman can fly; Lois does not believe Clark Kent can fly; therefore Superman is not Clark Kent.” The premises are true (in the story) and the conclusion false, so the argument form — applying Leibniz's Law to belief or doubt contexts — is not valid in its natural-language reading. The formal proof is valid only because the symbolisation treats “believed by Lois to fly” as an ordinary property, which it is not.

Exercise 13.2

Explain the difference between type and token identity theory, and how the distinction helps with multiple realisability.

Show solution

Type identity: each mental type (pain) is identical to a physical type (C-fibre firing). Token identity: each particular mental event is identical to some particular physical event, but not necessarily one of a common physical type. Multiple realisability counts against type identity (octopus pain is not C-fibre firing) but is consistent with token identity (each octopus pain is some physical event).

Exercise 13.3

Is the knowledge argument valid? Using the formalisation in this chapter, identify which premise the ability hypothesis rejects and explain why.

Show solution

It is valid (see the proof). The ability hypothesis says there is no fact Mary failed to know before release; what she lacked was know-how. So it rejects premise 2 (∃x¬Kx, read as “there is a fact Mary did not know”). It accepts that she gains something new, but denies that it is propositional knowledge of a fact.
