Analytic Philosophy: An Introduction with Symbolic Logic

A first-year course in analytic philosophy: what it is, how to argue, and the central debates about language, knowledge, reality, mind, morality, science and religion — built around a thorough grounding in deductive, symbolic logic.

Contents

Part I — What is analytic philosophy?

  • 1
    What analytic philosophy isFrege, Russell, Moore, Wittgenstein, the logical positivists, Quine, Kripke and ordinary-language philosophy; clarity, argument and analysis; the analytic–continental divide; how to read and write philosophy.
  • 2
    ArgumentsPremises and conclusions; deduction, induction and abduction; validity, soundness and cogency; reconstructing arguments; counterexamples; informal fallacies.

Part II — Deductive logic

  • 3
    Propositional logic: language and translationSentence letters and connectives, formation rules, scope, and translating English (“unless”, “only if”, “neither … nor”, exclusive “or”).
  • 4
    Truth tablesTruth-functional semantics; tautologies, contradictions and contingencies; equivalence and entailment; the short truth-table method; key equivalences; the paradoxes of material implication.
  • 5
    Natural deduction for propositional logicA Fitch-style system: introduction and elimination rules, subproofs, strategy, derived rules, many worked proofs; truth trees.
  • 6
    Predicate logicPredicates, names, variables and quantifiers; translating “all”, “some”, “no” and “only”; nested quantifiers; interpretations and counter-models; identity, counting and definite descriptions.
  • 7
    Natural deduction for predicate logic∀E, ∀I, ∃I, ∃E and their restrictions; identity rules; quantifier negation; worked proofs and common mistakes; trees for predicate logic.
  • 8
    A taste of metalogicSoundness and completeness, decidability and undecidability, compactness, Löwenheim–Skolem and Gödel’s incompleteness theorems — the ideas without the proofs.
  • 9
    Beyond classical logicModal logic and possible worlds; conditionals and counterfactuals; intuitionistic, relevance, paraconsistent and many-valued logics; logical pluralism.

Part III — Core areas of analytic philosophy

  • 10
    Language and meaningSense and reference, descriptions, names and rigid designation, speech acts, implicature, the two Wittgensteins, verificationism, indeterminacy of translation.
  • 11
    EpistemologyKnowledge and the Gettier problem, reliabilism, safety and sensitivity, virtue epistemology, scepticism, foundationalism and coherentism, the a priori, induction.
  • 12
    MetaphysicsIdentity and persistence, universals, causation, free will, time, modality, personal identity, ontology.
  • 13
    Philosophy of mindDualism, behaviourism, identity theory, functionalism, qualia, the Chinese room, mental causation, the hard problem.
  • 14
    Ethics and metaethicsConsequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics, trolley problems; the open question argument, expressivism, realism, is and ought.
  • 15
    Philosophy of science and religionFalsification, Kuhn, underdetermination, realism; ontological, cosmological and design arguments; the logical problem of evil.
  • 16
    Paradoxes and puzzlesThe liar, Russell’s paradox, the sorites, Newcomb’s problem, the Ship of Theseus and Zeno — and what logic can do with them.
