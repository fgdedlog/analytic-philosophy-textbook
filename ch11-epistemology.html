Epistemology

Learning objectives

  • state the justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge and explain how Gettier cases refute it;
  • describe and assess reliabilist, modal (sensitivity and safety) and virtue-theoretic responses;
  • formalise the closure-based sceptical argument and Moore's reply;
  • explain the regress problem and compare foundationalism and coherentism;
  • distinguish a priori from a posteriori and analytic from synthetic, and outline Quine's critique;
  • state Hume's problem of induction.

Epistemology is the theory of knowledge. Its core questions: What is knowledge? What justifies a belief? Can we know anything at all — and if so, how much? Epistemology is a natural home for the analytic method: proposed definitions are tested against cases, and arguments for scepticism are set out step by step.

The analysis of knowledge

We use “know” in several ways: knowing how (to swim), knowing by acquaintance (knowing Paris), and knowing that (knowing that Paris is in France). Epistemologists focus mostly on the last: propositional knowledge. What conditions must hold for a subject S to know that p?

Two conditions seem clearly necessary. Knowledge requires truth: you cannot know that the earth is flat, because it is not. And it requires belief: if you do not even believe p, you do not know it. But true belief is not enough. Someone who guesses the winner of a race and happens to be right does not know who would win. What is missing is a good reason — justification. This yields the traditional analysis, which goes back in some form to Plato's Theaetetus:

JTB analysis. S knows that p if and only if (i) p is true, (ii) S believes that p, and (iii) S is justified in believing that p.

Gettier cases

In a three-page paper, “Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?” (1963), Edmund Gettier presented two counterexamples. Here is the first. Smith and Jones have applied for the same job. Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get it (the company president told him so) and that Jones has ten coins in his pocket (Smith counted them). Smith infers: the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. In fact, unknown to Smith, Smith himself gets the job — and, also unknown to him, he too has ten coins in his pocket. So Smith's belief is true, and justified (validly inferred from well-supported premises). But Smith does not know it: it is true by luck, and what makes it true has nothing to do with his evidence.

A simpler modern example (adapted from Russell, who used a stopped clock): you look at a clock that reads 3:00 and form the belief that it is 3:00. The clock is reliable, and you have no reason to doubt it — but in fact it stopped exactly twelve hours ago. Your belief is justified and true, but not knowledge.

Logically, a single counterexample refutes a universal biconditional. If the analysis says that every case of justified true belief is a case of knowledge, then one case of justified true belief without knowledge contradicts it:

(Here the domain is cases of belief, Kx: x is a case of knowledge, and g is a Gettier case.) The JTB theorist must deny that the case is justified, true and believed, or accept that it is knowledge — or give up the analysis. Most philosophers gave up the analysis. Gettier cases share a structure: justification and truth come apart, and they are reconnected only by luck. The search for a fourth condition (or a replacement for justification) to rule out such luck became one of the major industries of twentieth-century epistemology.

Responses to Gettier

No false lemmas

Smith's belief was inferred from a false belief (that Jones will get the job). Perhaps knowledge requires that one's reasoning not depend on any falsehood. But some Gettier cases involve no inference at all. In Alvin Goldman's “fake barn” case (1976, crediting Carl Ginet), Henry drives through a district full of papier-mâché barn façades, indistinguishable from the road from real barns. By chance he looks at the one real barn and believes “that's a barn”. His belief is true, justified and not inferred from anything false — yet intuitively he does not know.

Reliabilism

Reliabilism (Goldman, “What Is Justified Belief?”, 1979) replaces justification-as-having-reasons with a condition on how the belief was formed: a belief is justified (or counts as knowledge) iff it was produced by a reliable process — one that tends to produce true beliefs, like normal perception or memory. Reliabilism is externalist: the factors that make a belief justified need not be accessible to the believer. This explains why young children and animals can know things without being able to articulate reasons.

Objections: (1) the generality problem — any belief is produced by a process that falls under many types (vision; vision in poor light; vision of barns from moving cars…), with different reliabilities, and there seems no principled way to choose; (2) cases like Laurence BonJour's Norman, a reliable clairvoyant who has no reason to think he has such a power, and so seems irresponsible rather than justified in trusting it; (3) the fake barn case: Henry's perceptual process is generally reliable, yet he lacks knowledge.

Sensitivity and safety

Modal conditions use counterfactuals (Chapter 9):

  • Sensitivity (Robert Nozick, Philosophical Explanations, 1981): S's belief that p is sensitive iff, if p were false, S would not believe p. Henry's belief fails: had he been looking at a fake, he would still have believed “that's a barn”.
  • Safety (Ernest Sosa, Timothy Williamson, Duncan Pritchard): S's belief is safe iff, in all nearby possible worlds in which S forms the belief in the same way, it is true. Henry's belief is unsafe: in nearby worlds he looks at a fake.

Sensitivity has a dramatic consequence. My belief that I am not a brain in a vat is insensitive: if I were a brain in a vat, I would still believe I wasn't. So, by the sensitivity condition, I do not know I am not a BIV — though I can know I have hands (if I had no hands I would notice). Nozick accepted that knowledge is therefore not closed under known entailment (see the next section). Safety theorists avoid this: in nearby worlds I am not envatted, so my belief that I am not a BIV is safe.

Virtue epistemology

Virtue epistemology (Sosa, Linda Zagzebski, John Greco) understands knowledge on the model of skilled performance. An archer's shot can be accurate (it hits), adroit (skilfully made) and apt (accurate because adroit). A gust of wind that blows a badly aimed arrow onto target yields an accurate but inapt shot. Knowledge, on Sosa's view, is apt belief: true because competent. Gettier subjects have beliefs that are true and competently formed, but not true because competently formed. (Whether fake-barn Henry's belief is apt is debated.)

Some philosophers draw a different lesson from the failure of analyses. Williamson (Knowledge and Its Limits, 2000) argues that knowledge is not analysable in terms of belief plus other conditions, but is a basic mental state in its own right — “knowledge first”.

Scepticism

Descartes and the brain in a vat

In the Meditations (1641), Descartes resolved to doubt everything that could possibly be doubted, to find a secure foundation for knowledge. The senses sometimes deceive; I might be dreaming; and, most radically, an evil demon might be deceiving me about everything. The modern version is the brain in a vat: perhaps your brain has been removed and connected to a computer that feeds you experiences exactly like those of a normal life. (Hilary Putnam discussed the scenario in Reason, Truth and History, 1981, arguing — controversially — that a brain in a vat could not truly think “I am a brain in a vat”, because its words would not refer to real brains and vats.)

The closure argument

The sceptic's argument relies on a closure principle: if S knows p, and S knows that p entails q, then S knows (or is in a position to know) q. Let h: I know I have hands; n: I know I am not a handless brain in a vat. Since I know that having hands entails not being a handless BIV, closure gives the first premise:

  1. P1If I know I have hands, I know I am not a brain in a vat. (closure)
  2. P2I do not know I am not a brain in a vat. (my experiences would be the same either way)
  3. CI do not know I have hands.

The argument is valid (modus tollens), so the anti-sceptic must reject a premise. There are three main options:

  • Deny closure (Nozick, Dretske): sensitivity holds for “I have hands” but not for “I am not a BIV”, so P1 is false.
  • Deny P2: Moore's response (below), or safety theory, or Putnam's semantic argument.
  • Contextualism (Keith DeRose, Stewart Cohen, David Lewis): the standards for “know” shift with context. In ordinary contexts, “I know I have hands” is true; in a philosophy seminar where sceptical possibilities are raised, the standards rise and it becomes false. Both the sceptic and the ordinary speaker speak truly by their own standards.

Moore's reply

G. E. Moore (“Proof of an External World”, 1939) held up his hands and said “Here is one hand, and here is another”, concluding that external objects exist. Against the sceptic, his strategy turns the argument around:

Moore and the sceptic share premise P1; they disagree about which of h and ¬n to accept. Moore claimed to be more certain that he had hands than he could be of any premise in a sceptical argument. As the slogan goes, one philosopher's modus ponens is another's modus tollens. Many find Moore's reply question-begging; others think it captures something right about the rational priority of common sense.

The structure of justification

The regress problem

Suppose belief A is justified by belief B. What justifies B? If another belief C, what justifies C? There seem to be only three options (sometimes called Agrippa's trilemma, after the ancient sceptic):

  1. the chain goes on forever (infinitism);
  2. the chain circles back on itself;
  3. the chain ends with beliefs that are justified but not by other beliefs.

The sceptic argues that each option is unacceptable, so no belief is justified.

Foundationalism

Foundationalism takes option 3: some beliefs are basic — justified non-inferentially, for example by experience or by self-evidence — and all other justified beliefs rest on them. Classical foundationalists (Descartes, and later the empiricists) required basic beliefs to be infallible or indubitable, such as beliefs about one's own current experiences. Modern “modest” foundationalists allow fallible basic beliefs, such as ordinary perceptual beliefs. The main challenge (pressed by Wilfrid Sellars in “Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind”, 1956, against “the Myth of the Given”): if an experience justifies a belief, it must have propositional content that can stand in logical relations — but then why does it not itself need justification?

Coherentism

Coherentism rejects the linear picture. A belief is justified by belonging to a coherent system of beliefs — one that is consistent, and whose members support and explain one another. Justification is holistic, like a web or (Neurath's image) a ship rebuilt at sea plank by plank. Objections: (1) the isolation objection — a coherent fairy tale could be entirely cut off from reality; (2) the alternative systems objection — many incompatible systems might be equally coherent. Coherentists reply that a system including beliefs about one's experiences will be constrained by the world. Susan Haack's foundherentism combines elements of both, using the analogy of a crossword puzzle (clues = experience; intersecting answers = coherence).

A priori and analytic

Two distinctions, often confused, must be kept apart:

  • An epistemological distinction: a proposition is a priori if it can be known independently of experience (beyond what is needed to acquire the concepts), and a posteriori otherwise. “7 + 5 = 12” is a priori; “there are tigers in India” is a posteriori.
  • A semantic distinction: a sentence is analytic if it is true in virtue of meaning alone (“all bachelors are unmarried”), synthetic otherwise.

(And a third, metaphysical, distinction between necessary and contingent truths: see Kripke in Chapter 10.) Kant argued that some knowledge is synthetic a priori — informative about the world yet knowable independently of experience — including mathematics and the principle that every event has a cause. The logical positivists, following Hume's distinction between “relations of ideas” and “matters of fact”, denied this: all a priori knowledge is analytic, and its necessity reflects only our linguistic conventions. Logic and mathematics, on this view, are true by definition and say nothing about the world.

Quine's critique

In “Two Dogmas of Empiricism” (1951), Quine attacked the analytic–synthetic distinction. Analyticity is usually explained via synonymy (“bachelor” means “unmarried man”), but synonymy, he argued, can only be explained in terms of analyticity, definition, or necessity — and these in turn presuppose synonymy. The circle of intensional notions never reaches anything independently clear. Quine's second target was reductionism: the idea that each statement has its own empirical content. Instead, “our statements about the external world face the tribunal of sense experience not individually but only as a corporate body”. Any statement can be held true come what may, if we make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere; and no statement — not even a law of logic — is immune from revision. (Quantum mechanics has been cited as a possible reason to revise classical logic.)

Replies: H. P. Grice and P. F. Strawson (“In Defense of a Dogma”, 1956) argued that a distinction that competent speakers draw with substantial agreement cannot be empty just because it resists definition in other terms. And many later philosophers think Kripke's work, together with developments in semantics, has rehabilitated modal and semantic notions. But Quine's holism remains influential.

The problem of induction

We believe the sun will rise tomorrow, that bread will nourish, that unsupported objects fall. These beliefs about the unobserved are based on past experience. But what justifies the inference? David Hume (A Treatise of Human Nature, 1739–40; An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, 1748) argued that it cannot be justified at all:

  1. P1Any justification of induction is either a demonstrative (deductive) argument or a probable (inductive) argument.
  2. P2No demonstrative argument can establish that the future will resemble the past, since it is conceivable, and so not contradictory, that it will not.
  3. P3Any probable argument for induction would itself rely on the principle that the future will resemble the past, and so beg the question.
  4. CInduction cannot be justified.

Hume's own conclusion was that we infer by custom or habit, not by reason. Responses include: Kant's view that causal order is a precondition of experience; Popper's attempt to do without induction altogether (Chapter 15); Strawson's claim that it is analytic that inductive reasoning is reasonable; probabilistic (Bayesian) approaches; and inference to the best explanation. Nelson Goodman's “new riddle of induction” (Fact, Fiction, and Forecast, 1955) made matters worse: define “grue” as “green if observed before 2050, blue otherwise”. All emeralds observed so far are green — and equally grue. Why project “green” rather than “grue”? The problem is not only whether induction is justified, but which inductions are.

Summary

  • The JTB analysis says knowledge is justified true belief; Gettier cases show these conditions are not sufficient.
  • Responses include no-false-lemmas, reliabilism, sensitivity, safety and virtue epistemology; some (Williamson) take knowledge to be unanalysable.
  • The closure-based sceptical argument is valid; anti-sceptics deny closure, deny the second premise (Moore), or adopt contextualism.
  • The regress problem motivates foundationalism (basic beliefs) and coherentism (mutual support).
  • A priori/a posteriori is epistemic; analytic/synthetic is semantic. Quine attacked the analytic–synthetic distinction and defended holism.
  • Hume argued that induction cannot be justified without circularity; Goodman's grue shows a further problem about which predicates to project.

Key terms

Epistemology
The theory of knowledge and justified belief.
Justified true belief
The traditional analysis of knowledge, challenged by Gettier.
Justification
Whatever makes a belief reasonable or well supported.
Gettier case
A case of justified true belief that is intuitively not knowledge, usually because the belief is true by luck.
Reliabilism
The view that a belief is justified (or knowledge) if produced by a reliable process.
Sensitivity
A belief that p is sensitive if, were p false, one would not believe p.
Safety
A belief is safe if, in nearby possible worlds where one forms it in the same way, it is true.
Virtue epistemology
The view that knowledge is true belief that results from the exercise of intellectual virtue or competence.
Scepticism
The view that we do not have knowledge (or justified belief) in some domain, e.g. about the external world.
Brain in a vat
A sceptical scenario in which one's brain is kept alive and fed experiences by a computer.
Foundationalism
The view that justified beliefs rest ultimately on basic beliefs that are not justified by other beliefs.
Coherentism
The view that beliefs are justified by fitting into a coherent system of beliefs, not by resting on foundations.
A priori
Knowable independently of sense experience (beyond what is needed to grasp the concepts), e.g. "7 + 5 = 12".
A posteriori
Knowable only on the basis of sense experience, e.g. "Water boils at 100 °C at sea level".
Analytic
True (or false) in virtue of meaning alone, e.g. "All bachelors are unmarried".
Synthetic
True or false not merely in virtue of meaning; telling us about the world.
Problem of induction
Hume's challenge: no non-circular justification can be given for expecting the future to resemble the past.

Exercises

Exercise 11.1

Construct your own Gettier case. Explain which conditions of the JTB analysis it satisfies and why it is not knowledge.

Show solution

Example: Mary sees what she takes to be her husband's car in the drive and believes he is home. The car is in fact a neighbour's identical car; but her husband is home, having arrived by taxi. Her belief is true, believed, and justified (seeing a familiar car is good evidence), but she does not know he is home, because what justifies her belief is disconnected from what makes it true.

Exercise 11.2

Explain how the sensitivity condition handles the fake-barn case, and why it leads Nozick to deny closure.

Show solution

Henry's belief “that's a barn” is insensitive: had it been a fake, he would still have believed it was a barn. So he doesn't know. Similarly my belief that I'm not a BIV is insensitive, so I don't know it; but my belief that I have hands is sensitive (in the nearest worlds where I lack hands — e.g. after an accident — I wouldn't believe I had them). So I know I have hands, I know that having hands entails not being a BIV, yet I don't know I'm not a BIV: closure fails.

Exercise 11.3

Formalise the argument: “If knowledge is possible, some beliefs are justified. If some beliefs are justified, then either there are basic beliefs or justification is circular or infinite. Justification is neither circular nor infinite, and there are no basic beliefs. So knowledge is not possible.” Is it valid? Prove it or give a counterexample.

Show solution

Key: k: knowledge is possible; j: some beliefs are justified; b: there are basic beliefs; c: justification is circular; i: justification is infinite. Valid:

Since it is valid, the foundationalist must reject the claim that there are no basic beliefs, the coherentist the claim that justification cannot be circular (in the holistic sense), and the infinitist the rejection of infinite chains.

Exercise 11.4

Classify each as a priori or a posteriori, and as analytic or synthetic (if you can): (a) All vixens are female foxes. (b) Water boils at 100 °C at sea level. (c) Every event has a cause. (d) 7 + 5 = 12. (e) Nothing can be red all over and green all over at the same time.

Show solution

(a) a priori, analytic. (b) a posteriori, synthetic. (c) controversial: Kant held it synthetic a priori; empiricists would call it synthetic and, if known at all, a posteriori — or deny it. (d) a priori; Kant said synthetic, logicists and positivists analytic. (e) a priori; a famous candidate for the synthetic a priori, though some argue it is analytic given the meanings of colour words.

Exercise 11.5

“Moore's proof begs the question.” Assess this claim.

Show solution

A good answer explains what begging the question is (assuming what is to be proved) and asks whether Moore assumes that the sceptic is wrong. Defenders note that Moore does not claim to persuade the sceptic, but to show that his own belief is more reasonable than any sceptical premise; the argument may be a perfectly good proof that fails to have dialectical force against someone who already doubts its premise (Crispin Wright and James Pryor have debated this under the heading of “transmission failure”).
