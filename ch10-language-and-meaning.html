Learning objectives

  • explain Frege's puzzle about identity and his distinction between sense and reference;
  • apply Russell's theory of descriptions to puzzles about empty names and informative identities;
  • set out Kripke's arguments against descriptivism and explain rigid designation and the causal picture of reference;
  • describe speech act theory and Grice's theory of conversational implicature;
  • contrast the early and later Wittgenstein; explain verificationism and Quine's indeterminacy of translation.

Analytic philosophy took a “linguistic turn” early in its history: many philosophers came to think that questions about thought and reality are best approached through questions about language. What is meaning? How do words hook onto the world? This chapter introduces the central ideas, and shows how the logic of Part II sharpens them.

Frege: sense and reference

The puzzle of informative identity

The ancient Babylonians called a bright heavenly body seen in the evening “Hesperus” and one seen in the morning “Phosphorus”. It was an astronomical discovery that Hesperus is Phosphorus: both are the planet Venus. Now compare:

  1. Hesperus is Hesperus.
  2. Hesperus is Phosphorus.

Sentence (1) is trivial and knowable a priori. Sentence (2) is informative: it records a discovery. But if the meaning of a name is just the object it stands for, the two sentences say the same thing — that a certain object is identical with itself. So why do they differ in what Frege called “cognitive value”? This is Frege's puzzle, posed at the start of “On Sense and Reference” (“Über Sinn und Bedeutung”, 1892).

Frege's solution

Frege distinguished two aspects of the meaning of an expression:

  • its reference (Bedeutung): the object it stands for — for both names, Venus;
  • its sense (Sinn): the “mode of presentation” of that object — roughly, the way the object is given to a thinker. “Hesperus” presents Venus as the evening star; “Phosphorus” as the morning star.

(1) and (2) have the same reference but different senses, which is why someone can understand both and yet accept one and doubt the other. Frege extended the distinction to whole sentences: the sense of a sentence is the thought it expresses (what is grasped, believed or doubted); its reference is its truth value. A key principle — compositionality — says the sense (and reference) of a complex expression is determined by the senses (references) of its parts.

Opaque contexts

Frege's theory also explains a failure of substitution. Leibniz's Law (=E in Chapter 7) licenses replacing co-referring names:

If Vx means “x is visible in the evening sky”, this is fine. But suppose Bx means “the ancient astronomer believed that x is visible in the evening”. Then Bh may be true and Bp false, though h = p. The inference that is formally valid in our logic fails. Belief reports create opaque (or “intensional”) contexts in which co-referring expressions cannot always be swapped while preserving truth. The lesson for formalisation: “believes that x is visible in the evening” is not a genuine predicate of the object x, and so should not be represented as a simple one-place predicate. Frege's own explanation was that in such contexts expressions refer to their customary senses, so “Hesperus” and “Phosphorus” have different referents there.

Frege also allowed that an expression can have sense without reference (“the least rapidly convergent series”, “Odysseus”). Sentences containing such expressions express thoughts but, he held, lack truth value.

Russell: descriptions and logical form

Russell rejected Frege's senses. In “On Denoting” (1905) he argued that many apparent names are really disguised definite descriptions, and that descriptions are not referring expressions at all but quantifier phrases. You met the analysis in Chapter 6: “The F is G” is ∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y = x) ∧ Gx).

This solved three puzzles at once:

  1. Empty descriptions. “The present King of France is bald” is meaningful and false, not truth-valueless, because it asserts the existence of a unique king.
  2. Negative existentials. “The round square does not exist” is true: ¬∃x(Rx ∧ Sx ∧ ∀y((Ry ∧ Sy) → y = x)). No mysterious non-existent object is needed for the sentence to be about.
  3. Informative identities. “Scott is the author of Waverley” says that exactly one thing wrote Waverley and that thing is Scott — not the trivial self-identity of Scott.

Russell went further, holding that ordinary proper names like “Socrates” are abbreviated descriptions (“the teacher of Plato”, say), and that only expressions for things we are directly acquainted with (perhaps “this”, or sense data) are “logically proper names”. The general moral — that the grammatical form of a sentence may differ from its logical form — inspired a whole programme of philosophical analysis. Frank Ramsey called the theory of descriptions “that paradigm of philosophy”.

Kripke: names and necessity

Descriptivism and its problems

The descriptivist view, associated with Frege and Russell and refined into a cluster theory of names by Searle and Strawson, holds that a name refers to whatever satisfies the descriptions (or most of a cluster of descriptions) that speakers associate with it. In Naming and Necessity (lectures 1970, published 1980), Kripke argued that descriptivism is false. Three arguments:

  • The modal argument. Suppose “Aristotle” means “the teacher of Alexander the Great”. Then “Aristotle was the teacher of Alexander” would be necessarily true — true in every possible world. But it is not: Aristotle might have died young and taught no one. So the name does not mean the description.
  • The epistemic argument. If “Gödel” meant “the man who proved the incompleteness of arithmetic”, then “Gödel proved incompleteness” would be knowable a priori. It is not.
  • The semantic argument. Suppose (Kripke's story) the incompleteness proof was really written by an unknown man called Schmidt, whose manuscript Gödel stole. Speakers whose only description of “Gödel” is “the prover of incompleteness” would, on descriptivism, be referring to Schmidt when they say “Gödel”. But they are not: they refer to Gödel, and say something false about him. Many speakers, moreover, associate no uniquely identifying description with a name at all (“Feynman — some physicist”) yet refer successfully.

The modal argument can be set out in standard form:

  1. P1If “Aristotle” meant “the teacher of Alexander”, then “Aristotle taught Alexander” would express a necessary truth.
  2. P2“Aristotle taught Alexander” does not express a necessary truth.
  3. C“Aristotle” does not mean “the teacher of Alexander”.

This is modus tollens, so it is valid; descriptivists must reject a premise. One reply (from Michael Dummett and others) is that the description could take “wide scope” over the modal operator, or be “rigidified” (“the actual teacher of Alexander”). The debate continues.

Rigid designation

Kripke's positive view uses possible worlds (Chapter 9). A rigid designator is an expression that refers to the same object in every possible world in which the object exists. Proper names are rigid: “Aristotle” picks out Aristotle even when we talk about worlds where he never taught. Most descriptions are not rigid: “the teacher of Alexander” picks out different people in different worlds.

A striking consequence: if “Hesperus” and “Phosphorus” are both rigid and co-refer, then “Hesperus is Phosphorus” is true in every world — necessary — though it was discovered empirically. So there are necessary a posteriori truths. The argument can be formalised in quantified modal logic: from ∀x□x = x (everything is necessarily self-identical) and Leibniz's Law one can derive ∀x∀y(x = y → □x = y) — the necessity of identity. Kripke also argued for necessary a posteriori truths about natural kinds (“water is H₂O”, “heat is molecular motion”) and for contingent a priori truths (“the standard metre bar is one metre long”, said by someone who fixes the reference of “one metre” by that bar). These claims broke the traditional assumption that the necessary, the a priori and the analytic coincide.

The causal picture

If not descriptions, what fixes reference? Kripke offered a “picture” rather than a theory: a name is introduced at an initial “baptism”, by pointing or by a reference-fixing description, and then passed from speaker to speaker, each intending to use it as the previous one did. A name refers to whatever lies at the origin of the chain. This causal theory of reference was extended to natural-kind terms by Hilary Putnam (“The Meaning of ‘Meaning’”, 1975), whose Twin Earth thought experiment suggests that “meanings just ain't in the head”: what “water” refers to depends on the actual stuff in our environment, not on what is in speakers' minds.

Speech acts

Words are not only for describing. J. L. Austin (How to Do Things with Words, 1962) began from performative utterances: “I promise to come”, “I name this ship the Queen Elizabeth”, “I bet you sixpence it will rain”. To say these things (in the right circumstances) is not to describe an action but to perform it. Such utterances are not true or false but can be “infelicitous” — misfiring if, say, the person naming the ship has no authority to do so.

Austin then generalised: every utterance performs several acts at once — a locutionary act (saying something with a certain meaning), an illocutionary act (what is done in saying it: asserting, warning, promising, ordering), and a perlocutionary act (what is brought about by saying it: persuading, frightening). John Searle systematised speech act theory (Speech Acts, 1969), classifying illocutionary acts and stating the conditions under which they succeed. One lesson for logic: the propositional content of an utterance can be the same across different forces — “Close the door”, “Will you close the door?”, “You will close the door” — and logic studies only one dimension of language use.

Grice: what is said and what is implicated

Ordinary-language philosophers often argued that logic misrepresents ordinary words: “and” sometimes means “and then”, “or” suggests the speaker does not know which, “some” suggests “not all”. Paul Grice (“Logic and Conversation”, 1975, from his 1967 William James lectures) gave a powerful reply. He distinguished what is said — the literal truth-conditional content — from what is implicated.

Conversation, Grice proposed, is governed by a cooperative principle: make your contribution such as is required by the accepted purpose of the conversation. It has four maxims:

  • Quantity: be as informative as required, and no more.
  • Quality: try to say only what you believe true and have evidence for.
  • Relation: be relevant.
  • Manner: be clear, brief and orderly; avoid ambiguity and obscurity.

Hearers assume the speaker is cooperative and draw inferences accordingly. If I say “Some of the students passed”, you infer that not all did — because if all had, the maxim of Quantity would have required me to say so. But “not all” is not part of what I said: I can cancel it without contradiction (“Some of the students passed — in fact all of them did”). Cancellability is the test that distinguishes conversational implicatures from literal content.

Implicature at work

  • “She got married and had a baby” implicates temporal order (Manner: be orderly), but m ∧ b is logically equivalent to b ∧ m.
  • “Either the money is in the safe or it's in the drawer” implicates that I do not know which (Quantity), but the disjunction is true if I know it is in the safe.
  • A reference letter that says only “Mr X has excellent handwriting and attended regularly” implicates that he is a poor philosopher (Quantity and Relation, flouted).

Grice's distinction protects the logical analysis of the connectives: the apparent differences between “and”, “or”, “if” and their formal counterparts can be explained pragmatically. As we saw in Chapter 4, he applied this to the paradoxes of material implication.

Wittgenstein: from picture to use

In the Tractatus (1921), Wittgenstein held that the world is the totality of facts; elementary propositions are pictures of possible atomic facts, sharing their logical form; and every meaningful proposition is a truth function of elementary propositions. Logical truths (tautologies) say nothing — they are true however the world is. Ethics, aesthetics and the meaning of life cannot be put into words; they “show themselves”. Philosophy is not a body of doctrine but an activity of clarification, and the Tractatus itself, by its own standards, consists of nonsense — a ladder to be thrown away after climbing it. “Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.”

In the Philosophical Investigations (1953), Wittgenstein attacked the picture theory, including his own earlier version. Words do not all name things; language is a motley of activities — giving orders, describing, reporting, speculating, making jokes, thanking, praying — which he called language games. “For a large class of cases … the meaning of a word is its use in the language” (§43). Many concepts are not defined by necessary and sufficient conditions but unified by family resemblances — consider “game”. And in the famous rule-following considerations and “private language argument”, he questioned whether meaning could be fixed by anything purely inner, such as a mental image or a private ostensive definition. Saul Kripke's Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language (1982) developed this into a sceptical challenge about what makes it the case that we mean one thing rather than another.

Verificationism

The logical positivists' verification principle (Chapter 1) was a theory of meaning: a sentence is meaningful iff it is analytic or empirically verifiable, and the meaning of a synthetic sentence is its method of verification. A. J. Ayer's version (Language, Truth and Logic, 1936) declared metaphysics, theology and ethics literally meaningless.

The principle faced a dilemma. A strong version (conclusive verifiability) rules out general scientific laws, which no finite observation can conclusively verify. A weak version (some observation must be relevant) proved almost impossible to state without letting everything in. Ayer's first-edition formulation, for instance — roughly, S is meaningful if together with some auxiliary premises it entails an observation sentence not entailed by the auxiliaries alone — was quickly shown to let in almost any sentence; and Alonzo Church (1949) showed that Ayer's more careful revision in the 1946 second edition had the same defect. And the principle seemed to fail its own test. Still, the verificationist idea that meaning is connected to evidence survives in subtler forms, for example in Dummett's anti-realism (Chapter 9).

Quine: indeterminacy of translation

In Word and Object (1960), Quine imagined a linguist translating a completely unknown language with no help but the speakers' behaviour. A rabbit scurries by; a native says “Gavagai”. The linguist tentatively translates “Rabbit”. But, Quine argued, all possible behavioural evidence would equally fit translating “gavagai” as “undetached rabbit part” or “temporal stage of a rabbit” or “rabbithood manifesting itself”, provided compensating adjustments are made elsewhere in the translation manual (for example, in how we translate words for identity and counting). This is the indeterminacy of translation: rival translation manuals can fit all the evidence while disagreeing — and, Quine concluded, there is no further fact of the matter about which is correct.

Quine's thesis follows from his behaviourism about meaning combined with meaning holism: sentences have empirical content only as parts of whole theories. If correct, it undermines the idea that meanings are determinate entities — and with it the notion of synonymy on which the analytic–synthetic distinction depends (Chapter 11). Critics reply that the argument depends on an implausibly narrow behaviourism, and that we have good reason to prefer simpler, more natural translations.

Summary

  • Frege distinguished the sense of an expression from its reference, explaining informative identities and substitution failures in belief contexts.
  • Russell analysed definite descriptions as quantifier phrases, solving puzzles about empty descriptions and showing that grammatical form can mislead.
  • Kripke argued that names are rigid designators whose reference is fixed causally, not descriptively, and that there are necessary a posteriori truths.
  • Austin and Searle studied the acts performed in speaking; Grice distinguished what is said from what is implicated, defending the logical analysis of connectives.
  • The early Wittgenstein held a picture theory; the later Wittgenstein saw meaning as use within language games.
  • Verificationism tied meaning to verification but could not be satisfactorily formulated; Quine argued that translation is indeterminate.

Key terms

Sense
Frege's Sinn: the mode of presentation of a referent; what a competent speaker grasps.
Referent
The object a name or other expression picks out.
Compositionality
The principle that the meaning of a complex expression is determined by the meanings of its parts and their arrangement.
Definite description
A phrase of the form "the F", e.g. "the present King of France".
Logical form
The structure of a sentence or argument relevant to validity, abstracting from subject matter.
Cluster theory of names
The view that a name's reference is fixed by a cluster of descriptions speakers associate with it.
Rigid designator
An expression that refers to the same object in every possible world in which that object exists (Kripke).
Causal theory of reference
The view (Kripke, Putnam) that a name refers to whatever stands at the start of the causal chain of its uses.
Speech act
An action performed in speaking, such as asserting, promising or ordering.
Performative
An utterance that does something rather than describing, e.g. "I promise".
Implicature
What a speaker communicates beyond what is literally said, e.g. by exploiting Grice's maxims.
Cooperative principle
Grice's principle that conversational contributions should be such as is required by the purpose of the talk exchange.
Language game
Wittgenstein's term for a practice of language use embedded in a form of life.
Verification principle
The logical positivists' criterion: a statement is meaningful only if it is analytic or empirically verifiable.
Indeterminacy of translation
Quine's thesis that rival translation manuals can fit all possible evidence yet disagree.
Meaning holism
The view that the meaning of an expression depends on its place in a whole language or theory.

Exercises

Exercise 10.1

Explain Frege's puzzle and his solution. Then explain why “Lois Lane believes that Superman can fly” and “Lois Lane believes that Clark Kent can fly” can differ in truth value.

Show solution

“Superman = Clark Kent” is informative while “Superman = Superman” is trivial, though both concern one object. Frege: the names share a referent but differ in sense (mode of presentation). In belief reports, what matters is how Lois thinks of the object: she grasps the thought expressed by “Superman can fly” and accepts it, but not the different thought expressed by “Clark Kent can fly”. On Frege's view, in the belief context the names refer to their senses, which differ, so substitution need not preserve truth.

Exercise 10.2

Give Russellian analyses of (a) “The author of Middlemarch was a woman”, and (b) both readings of “The author of Middlemarch was not a man.” (Key: Ax: x wrote Middlemarch; Wx: x was a woman; Mx: x was a man.)

Show solution

(a) ∃x(Ax ∧ ∀y(Ay → y = x) ∧ Wx). (b) Narrow-scope negation: ∃x(Ax ∧ ∀y(Ay → y = x) ∧ ¬Mx); wide-scope: ¬∃x(Ax ∧ ∀y(Ay → y = x) ∧ Mx). Since Middlemarch has a unique author (George Eliot, i.e. Mary Ann Evans), both readings are true here; they would come apart if there were no unique author.

Exercise 10.3

Set out Kripke's modal argument against descriptivism in premise–conclusion form. Which premise would a descriptivist most plausibly reject, and how?

Show solution

See the reconstruction in the chapter (a modus tollens). A descriptivist might reject P1 by holding that the description associated with the name takes wide scope over modal operators, or is rigidified by “actually” (“the actual teacher of Alexander”), so that “Aristotle taught Alexander” is not necessary even if the name abbreviates that description. Kripke and others reply that this concedes rigidity and faces the epistemic and semantic arguments anyway.

Exercise 10.4

Identify the implicature and the maxim involved: (a) A: “Is there petrol nearby?” B: “There's a garage round the corner.” (b) “I ate some of the biscuits.” (c) A: “How was the concert?” B: “The hall was very nicely decorated.”

Show solution

(a) The garage is open and sells petrol (Relation). (b) I did not eat all of them (Quantity). (c) The concert was not good (Quantity/Relation, flouted: B conspicuously avoids the question). Each can be cancelled (“… but it's closed”, “… in fact all of them”, “… and the music was superb too”), confirming they are implicatures rather than what was said.

Exercise 10.5

Is “Water is H₂O” necessary? Is it a priori? Explain Kripke's view and one objection.

Show solution

Kripke: “water” is a rigid designator for a natural kind whose essence is its molecular structure, so in every world water is H₂O — necessary; but this was discovered empirically — a posteriori. Objection: perhaps “water” is associated with a description (“the clear, drinkable stuff in our rivers”) so that in worlds where XYZ plays that role, “water” would refer to XYZ; two-dimensional semantics (Chalmers, Jackson) tries to capture both intuitions by distinguishing two kinds of intension. Others question whether ordinary “water” really is H₂O, since what we call water contains impurities and the chemistry is more complex than the slogan suggests.

Exercise 10.6

“Quine's gavagai argument shows that we can never really understand each other.” Discuss.

Show solution

A good answer explains the argument and notes that Quine's conclusion is not that communication fails but that there is no fact of the matter about meaning beyond what behaviour fixes; communication proceeds smoothly because we share dispositions. It might discuss whether indeterminacy applies at home (Quine said yes), and objections: the argument presupposes behaviourism, and considerations of simplicity or naturalness (or innate cognitive structure, as Chomsky argued) may privilege one translation.
