Learning objectives

  • read and translate formulas of propositional modal logic, and evaluate them in Kripke models;
  • connect the modal systems K, T, S4 and S5 with properties of the accessibility relation;
  • distinguish material, strict and counterfactual conditionals, and outline the Lewis–Stalnaker semantics;
  • explain the motivations for intuitionistic, relevance, paraconsistent and many-valued logics, and which classical principles each rejects;
  • describe the debate between logical monism and logical pluralism.

Classical logic — the logic of Chapters 3–8 — is the standard. But it is not the only logic, and philosophers have developed alternatives for two kinds of reason. Some logics extend classical logic with new vocabulary (necessity, time, knowledge, obligation) while keeping its principles. Others are rivals: they reject some classical principle — excluded middle, explosion, bivalence — as incorrect, at least in some domain. This chapter surveys both.

Modal logic

Necessity and possibility

Some truths seem to hold necessarily (2 + 2 = 4; all bachelors are unmarried), others only contingently (Paris is the capital of France). Modal logic adds two operators to propositional logic:

  • □A: “necessarily A” (it must be that A);
  • ◇A: “possibly A” (it might be that A).

They are interdefinable: something is possible iff its negation is not necessary, and necessary iff its negation is not possible.

◇A ⇔ ¬□¬A    □A ⇔ ¬◇¬A

These operators are not truth-functional: knowing that p is true does not tell you whether □p is. So truth tables cannot give their meaning. There are also many kinds of necessity — logical, metaphysical, physical, epistemic (“for all I know”), deontic (“it is obligatory that”) — and different modal logics suit different kinds.

Possible worlds

The key idea, going back to Leibniz and made precise by Kripke (and independently by others, including Stig Kanger and Jaakko Hintikka) between about 1957 and 1963, is to think of modal claims as quantifying over possible worlds — complete ways things might have been.

  • □A is true iff A is true in every possible world;
  • ◇A is true iff A is true in at least one possible world.

This explains the duality above: it mirrors ∃x ⇔ ¬∀x¬. Necessity is a universal quantifier over worlds; possibility an existential one.

Kripke semantics

Kripke's refinement is to make necessity relative: what is possible may depend on which world you are in. A Kripke model has:

  1. a non-empty set W of worlds;
  2. an accessibility relation R on W (wRv: world v is possible relative to w);
  3. a valuation saying which sentence letters are true at which worlds.

Then, at a world w: □A is true iff A is true at every world accessible from w; ◇A is true iff A is true at some world accessible from w. Connectives work world by world, as usual. A formula is valid in a class of models if it is true at every world of every model in the class.

A small model

W = {w1, w2, w3}; R = {⟨w1, w2⟩, ⟨w1, w3⟩, ⟨w2, w2⟩}; p is true at w2 and w3; q is true at w2 only.

  • At w1: □p is true (p holds at w2 and w3, the worlds accessible from w1), though p itself is false at w1. ◇q is true (via w2); □q is false (w3).
  • At w3: no world is accessible, so □A is (vacuously) true for every A — even □⊥ — and ◇A is false for every A.

Systems and frame conditions

Different modal logics correspond to different conditions on R. The weakest normal modal logic, K (for Kripke), is valid in all models. Its characteristic principle is the distribution axiom:

K: □(A → B) → (□A → □B)

together with the necessitation rule: if A is a theorem, so is □A. Stronger systems add axioms, each matching a property of R:

AxiomFormulaCondition on RIntuition

5◇A → □◇Aeuclideanwhat is possible is necessarily possible
D□A → ◇Aserial (every world sees some world)what is obligatory is permitted (deontic logic)

The famous systems: T = K + T; S4 = K + T + 4 (reflexive, transitive); S5 = K + T + 5 (equivalently K + T + B + 4; R is an equivalence relation). In S5 every world sees every world in its class, so the modal facts are the same everywhere, and iterated modalities collapse: ◇□A is equivalent to □A. S5 is widely regarded as the logic of metaphysical necessity; S4 has been proposed as a logic of knowledge (Hintikka) and is closely linked to intuitionistic logic (Gödel); D-systems are used for obligation (where T is clearly wrong — not everything obligatory happens).

A modal tableau

Truth trees extend to modal logic by labelling formulas with worlds. To refute □A at world w (i.e. when we have ¬□A at w), introduce a new world v with wRv and put ¬A at v; when □A holds at w, put A at every world accessible from w. Here is a K-tableau showing that the K axiom is valid. We assume its negation at world 0 and try to build a counter-model:

10¬p, 1from 8× (9, 10)
10q, 1from 8× (7, 10)

Both branches close, so no counter-model exists: K is valid in every Kripke model. By contrast, try □p → p (axiom T). Assume □p and ¬p at world 0. If no world is accessible from 0, □p holds vacuously, and nothing forces p at 0. The branch stays open, giving a counter-model — unless we require R to be reflexive, in which case 0R0 gives p at 0 and the branch closes. That is exactly why T corresponds to reflexivity.

The possible-worlds clauses can be written in first-order logic (the “standard translation”): treat worlds as objects, R as a two-place predicate, and each sentence letter as a one-place predicate of worlds. □p → p at world a becomes ∀x(Rax → Px) → Pa. In this interpretation
  • ∀x(Rax → Px) → Pa is false
  • ∀x(Rax → Px) is true
  • ∃x(Rax ∧ Px) is true

(The last line shows that ◇p is true at world 0 too.)

Quantified modal logic and the Barcan formula

Combining modal operators with quantifiers raises philosophical questions. Is the Barcan formula ◇∃xFx → ∃x◇Fx valid? (“If there could have been a unicorn, then there is something that could have been a unicorn.”) It is named after Ruth Barcan Marcus, who first studied quantified modal logic in 1946. In Kripke semantics it holds when no accessible world contains objects that the current world lacks — in particular, when every world has the same domain. Its converse, ∃x◇Fx → ◇∃xFx, holds when objects never go missing as we move to accessible worlds. Whether there are merely possible objects, and so whether domains can vary, is a question in the metaphysics of modality (Chapter 12).

Quick check In Kripke semantics, which condition on the accessibility relation validates □A → A?

If every world can see itself, then whatever holds at all accessible worlds holds at the world itself.

Conditionals

Chapter 4 ended with doubts about the material conditional. Modal logic provides alternatives.

Strict implication

C. I. Lewis (1912, 1918) proposed the strict conditional: “if A then B” means □(A → B) — necessarily, if A then B. This avoids the paradoxes of material implication: a true consequent or false antecedent no longer makes the conditional true. But it has its own “paradoxes of strict implication”: an impossible antecedent strictly implies everything, and a necessary consequent is strictly implied by everything. And many everyday conditionals (“if it rains, the match will be off”) are not necessary at all.

Counterfactuals

A counterfactual is a subjunctive conditional whose antecedent is (typically) false: “If I had struck this match, it would have lit.” We write A □→ B. Counterfactuals are neither material (otherwise all those with false antecedents would be true) nor strict (it is not necessary that a struck match lights: it might have been wet).

The influential semantics of Robert Stalnaker (1968) and David Lewis (Counterfactuals, 1973) appeals to similarity between worlds. Roughly:

A □→ B is true iff, in the closest (most similar) worlds in which A is true, B is also true.

(Stalnaker assumes there is a unique closest A-world; Lewis allows ties and handles the case where there is no closest world.) The struck-match counterfactual is true because the most similar worlds in which I strike the match are ones where it is dry and oxygen is present, and there it lights.

This semantics invalidates several patterns that are valid for the material conditional — and rightly so:

  • Strengthening the antecedent fails: “If I had struck the match, it would have lit” does not entail “If I had struck the match and it had been soaked in water, it would have lit.”
  • Transitivity fails: “If Hoover had been a communist, he would have been a traitor; if Hoover had been born in Russia, he would have been a communist” do not together yield “If Hoover had been born in Russia, he would have been a traitor” (a classic example adapted from Stalnaker).
  • Contraposition fails: “If it had rained, there would not have been a heavy downpour” could be true (it might only have drizzled), but “If there had been a heavy downpour, it would not have rained” is absurd.

Counterfactuals matter throughout philosophy: in theories of causation (Chapter 12), of knowledge (sensitivity and safety, Chapter 11), of dispositions and laws of nature. Indicative conditionals (“If Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did”, which is true) are a further puzzle; whether they are material, have Stalnaker-style truth conditions, or lack truth conditions altogether remains debated.

Intuitionistic logic

Intuitionistic logic grew out of L. E. J. Brouwer's constructivist philosophy of mathematics and was formalised by Arend Heyting (1930). For the intuitionist, mathematical truth is not correspondence to a mind-independent realm but the existence of a proof (a mental construction). The meaning of the connectives is given by what counts as a proof of them — the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov (BHK) interpretation:

  • a proof of A ∧ B is a proof of A together with a proof of B;
  • a proof of A ∨ B is a proof of A or a proof of B, together with an indication of which;
  • a proof of A → B is a method that turns any proof of A into a proof of B;
  • a proof of ¬A is a method that turns any proof of A into a proof of ⊥ (absurdity);
  • a proof of ∃xA(x) is a particular object c together with a proof of A(c).

On this reading, the law of excluded middle A ∨ ¬A is not generally acceptable: it would require, for every A, either a proof of A or a proof that A is refutable — and for open problems (such as Goldbach's conjecture) we have neither. Likewise double-negation elimination (¬¬A → A) fails: refuting the refutability of A does not construct a proof of A. In our natural-deduction system, intuitionistic logic is obtained by dropping IP (and the derived rules DNE and TND) while keeping ¬I, ¬E and X. Every intuitionistically valid argument is classically valid, but not conversely.

Some things remain intuitionistically provable: A → ¬¬A, ¬¬¬A → ¬A, and even ¬¬(A ∨ ¬A) — the double negation of excluded middle. (Try the last in the proof checker with derived rules switched off and without using IP. Hint: assume ¬(p ∨ ¬p), derive ¬p by ¬I, then p ∨ ¬p by ∨I, and close with ¬I.)

Michael Dummett generalised the intuitionist's argument beyond mathematics: if meaning is tied to the conditions under which we can recognise a sentence as warranted, we may lack grounds for asserting bivalence for statements about the remote past or about undetectable states — a position he called anti-realism.

Relevance logic

In classical logic, p ∧ ¬p ⊨ q and q ⊨ p → p: premises need have nothing to do with the conclusion. Relevance logic (or relevant logic), developed by Alan Ross Anderson and Nuel Belnap (Entailment, 1975) and others, requires that premises be relevant to the conclusion. A common necessary condition is variable sharing: in a valid entailment A → B, A and B must share a sentence letter.

The most famous casualty is explosion. Here is C. I. Lewis's “independent proof” that a contradiction entails anything:

  1. p ∧ ¬p (premise)
  2. p (∧E, 1)
  3. p ∨ q (∨I, 2)
  4. ¬p (∧E, 1)
  5. q (disjunctive syllogism, 3, 4)

Each step looks innocent. Relevance logicians typically reject the last step: disjunctive syllogism is not valid in general (though it may be acceptable when we know the premises are consistent). Relevance logics also reject the paradoxes of material implication and use a non-truth-functional conditional, usually given a semantics with a three-place accessibility relation (Routley and Meyer) or with “impossible worlds”.

Paraconsistent logic

A logic is paraconsistent if explosion fails in it: an inconsistent set of premises does not entail everything. This matters whenever we must reason sensibly with inconsistent information — a database with conflicting entries, a legal code with conflicting clauses, a scientific theory known to be inconsistent (such as early versions of the calculus, or Bohr's theory of the atom).

A simple paraconsistent logic is Graham Priest's Logic of Paradox (LP, 1979). It has three values: T (true only), F (false only) and B (both true and false). Negation swaps T and F and leaves B fixed; conjunction takes the “lower” and disjunction the “higher” value in the order F < B < T. An argument is valid if, whenever all premises are T or B (“at least true”), so is the conclusion. Then p ∧ ¬p ⊭ q: let p = B (so p ∧ ¬p = B, at least true) and q = F.

Most paraconsistent logicians use such logics as tools without believing any contradiction is actually true. Dialetheists, notably Priest, go further: they hold that some contradictions are true — for instance, the liar sentence is both true and false (Chapter 16).

Many-valued logics

Classical logic is bivalent: every sentence is true or false. Many-valued logics drop bivalence.

  • Łukasiewicz (1920) introduced a third value, I (“indeterminate”), for future contingents, inspired by Aristotle's sea-battle argument: “there will be a sea battle tomorrow” is, he thought, not yet true or false.
  • Kleene's strong three-valued logic (K3, 1938) treats the third value as “undefined” (as with a computation that fails to halt). Its tables for ¬, ∧, ∨ are the same as LP's, but only T is designated. In K3, p ∨ ¬p is not a tautology (it takes value I when p does) — indeed K3 has no tautologies at all. Łukasiewicz's logic differs in its conditional: with p = q = I it makes p → q true, so p → p remains a tautology.
  • Fuzzy logics use infinitely many values — all real numbers between 0 and 1 — as degrees of truth. They have been applied to vagueness: “Ann is tall” might be true to degree 0.7. (See the sorites paradox, Chapter 16.)

Logical pluralism and monism

Faced with this variety, is there one correct logic? Logical monists say yes. Classical monists (Timothy Williamson, among others) argue that classical logic is the best overall theory — simple, strong and well tested in mathematics — and that apparent counterexamples are better handled elsewhere. Non-classical monists (such as Priest in some writings, or committed intuitionists) say the one true logic is non-classical. Quine argued that a “deviant” logician merely changes the subject: “change of logic, change of subject” — if you deny excluded middle, you no longer mean by “not” what classical logicians mean. Critics reply that this makes genuine disagreement about logic impossible, which seems wrong.

Logical pluralists, notably JC Beall and Greg Restall (Logical Pluralism, 2006), hold that there is more than one correct consequence relation. On their account, validity means truth-preservation in all cases, and different precise notions of “case” — complete and consistent possible worlds (classical logic), incomplete constructions (intuitionistic logic), inconsistent situations (relevance logic) — give different, equally legitimate logics. Others are pluralists in a more instrumental sense: different logics are useful tools for different jobs.

Whatever one's view, it is worth noticing that the debate itself is conducted with argument and counterexample — and that knowing classical logic well is the precondition for understanding its rivals.

Summary

  • Modal logic adds □ and ◇. In Kripke semantics, □A is true at a world iff A is true at every accessible world. K is valid in all models; T, 4, B, 5 correspond to reflexivity, transitivity, symmetry and euclideanness. S5 suits metaphysical necessity.
  • Strict conditionals (□(A → B)) avoid the paradoxes of material implication but face their own. The Lewis–Stalnaker semantics treats counterfactuals via the closest antecedent-worlds; strengthening, transitivity and contraposition fail.
  • Intuitionistic logic, based on proof rather than truth, rejects excluded middle and double-negation elimination (drop IP).
  • Relevance logic requires relevance between premises and conclusion and rejects explosion, usually by rejecting disjunctive syllogism.
  • Paraconsistent logics such as LP block explosion; dialetheists hold that some contradictions are true.
  • Many-valued logics (Łukasiewicz, Kleene, fuzzy) drop bivalence.
  • Monists hold there is one correct logic; pluralists (Beall and Restall) that there are several.

Key terms

Modal logic
The logic of necessity (□) and possibility (◇).
Possible world
A complete way things could have been; used to analyse necessity and possibility.
Accessibility relation
In Kripke semantics, the relation that says which possible worlds are "possible relative to" a given world.
Kripke semantics
Possible-worlds semantics for modal logic using worlds and an accessibility relation.
Strict conditional
□(A → B): necessarily, if A then B.
Counterfactual
A conditional about what would have happened had things been otherwise: "If A had been the case, B would have been".
Intuitionistic logic
A logic that rejects the law of excluded middle and double-negation elimination, treating truth as provability.
Relevance logic
A logic requiring that premises be relevant to the conclusion; it rejects explosion and the paradoxes of material implication.
Paraconsistent logic
A logic in which a contradiction does not entail every sentence.
Many-valued logic
A logic with more than two truth values.
Logical pluralism
The view that there is more than one correct logic (or consequence relation).
Law of excluded middle
The principle that every sentence of the form A ∨ ¬A is true.

Exercises

Exercise 9.1

Translate, using p: it rains: (a) It might rain. (b) It is necessary that it does not rain. (c) It is not necessary that it rains. (d) It is possible that it does not rain. Which pairs are equivalent?

Show solution

(a) ◇p. (b) □¬p. (c) ¬□p. (d) ◇¬p. (c) and (d) are equivalent; (a) and (b) are contradictories (each is equivalent to the negation of the other).

Exercise 9.2

In the model W = {w1, w2}, R = {⟨w1, w2⟩, ⟨w2, w2⟩}, with p true only at w2: what are the truth values at w1 of (a) p, (b) □p, (c) □□p, (d) □p → p? What does this show about axiom T?

Show solution

(a) F. (b) T (the only world accessible from w1 is w2, where p is true). (c) T (□p is true at w2, the only world w1 sees). (d) F. So T fails in this model; the relation is not reflexive (w1 does not see itself).

Exercise 9.3

Give an everyday example showing that “strengthening the antecedent” fails for counterfactuals.

Show solution

“If I had dropped the glass, it would have broken” may be true; “If I had dropped the glass and it had landed on a cushion, it would have broken” is false. The closest worlds where I drop the glass are not ones where a cushion happens to be beneath it.

Exercise 9.4

Show that ¬¬(p ∨ ¬p) can be proved without IP, DNE or TND (so it is intuitionistically valid).

Show solution

Only ∨I, ¬E and ¬I are used. Compare the classical proof of p ∨ ¬p in Chapter 5, which ends with IP instead of ¬I.

Exercise 9.5

In LP, show that disjunctive syllogism (p ∨ q, ¬p ⊨ q) fails.

Show solution

Let p = B and q = F. Then p ∨ q = B (designated) and ¬p = B (designated), but q = F (not designated). So the premises are at least true and the conclusion is not.

Exercise 9.6

Briefly assess Quine's slogan “change of logic, change of subject”.

Show solution

A good answer explains the slogan (a deviant logician who rejects, say, excluded middle must mean something different by “not” or “or”, so is not really contradicting the classical logician) and evaluates it: if correct, logical disagreement would be merely verbal, yet intuitionists and classical logicians seem to disagree about something substantive — e.g. whether certain mathematical proofs establish their conclusions. One might also note that the connectives' meanings could be fixed by shared core rules (such as ∧I/∧E) while disagreement concerns contested ones, or that Quine's own holism (any belief, even logic, is revisable) sits uneasily with the slogan.
