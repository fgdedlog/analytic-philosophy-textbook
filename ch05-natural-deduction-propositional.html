Learning objectives

  • explain what a formal proof is and why we need proofs as well as truth tables;
  • state and apply the introduction and elimination rules of a Fitch-style natural-deduction system;
  • use subproofs correctly: make assumptions, discharge them, and respect scope;
  • find proofs using systematic strategies;
  • use and justify derived rules;
  • test arguments with truth trees.

Why proofs?

Truth tables give a complete, mechanical test of validity in propositional logic. Why bother with anything else? Three reasons.

  1. Size. A truth table for an argument with 20 sentence letters has over a million rows. A proof may be a dozen lines.
  2. Understanding. A truth table tells you that an argument is valid; a proof shows why, by breaking the inference into small, obviously valid steps. Proofs model how we actually reason.
  3. Predicate logic. When we add quantifiers (Chapters 6–7), truth tables are no longer available: there are infinitely many interpretations to check. Proofs still work.

A proof is a finite sequence of formulas, each of which is either a premise, an assumption, or follows from earlier lines by a rule of inference. The system we use is a version of natural deduction, developed independently by Gerhard Gentzen and Stanisław Jaśkowski in 1934, in the graphical layout introduced by Frederic Fitch. It closely follows the system in the free textbook forall x (Magnus and Button). Each connective has an introduction rule (how to prove a formula with that main connective) and an elimination rule (how to use one).

We write A1, …, An ⊢ C to say that C can be proved from premises A1, …, An. Compare ⊨, which says that the premises entail the conclusion (no row makes them true and it false). That ⊢ and ⊨ coincide — everything provable is valid and everything valid is provable — is a deep result discussed in Chapter 8.

The rules for ∧ and →

Conjunction

∧I: if you have A and you have B, you may write A ∧ B (in either order). ∧E: from A ∧ B you may write A, or B.

Conditional elimination (modus ponens)

→E: from A → B and A, write B. (Some books call this MP, and the checker accepts that name.)

Note what →E does not allow: from A → B and B you cannot infer A (affirming the consequent).

Subproofs and assumptions

How can we prove a conditional? In ordinary reasoning we say: “Suppose A. Then … so B. Therefore, if A then B.” Natural deduction formalises this with a subproof. We make an assumption — a formula we suppose for the sake of argument, justified “AS” — and indent everything that depends on it with a new vertical bar. When we have derived what we want, we close the subproof and write a conclusion outside it that no longer depends on the assumption. Closing a subproof is called discharging the assumption.

Conditional introduction

→I: if you assume A and, within that subproof, derive B, you may close the subproof and write A → B. Cite the whole subproof as a range.

Worked proof: hypothetical syllogism

We want a conditional, so we assume its antecedent p and aim for its consequent r. Inside the subproof we may use the premises (lines 1 and 2) as well as the assumption. Having reached r, we close the subproof and conclude p → r on line 6, which depends only on the premises.

The rules of scope

Subproofs come with three rules that make the system sound. They are what the vertical bars are for.

  1. Access. At any line you may cite an earlier line only if it is in the same subproof or in a subproof that encloses the current one (one whose bar is still running). Once a subproof is closed, its individual lines are off limits. You may cite the closed subproof as a whole, by its range, with rules such as →I.
  2. Closure. A subproof may be closed at any point; the next line simply returns to the outer bar.
  3. Completion. A proof is finished only when the conclusion is on the main (outermost) bar. A conclusion still inside a subproof depends on an undischarged assumption.

Why the access rule? Lines inside a subproof depend on the assumption, which might be false. In the proof above, r on line 5 was derived only on the supposition that p. If we could cite line 5 after the subproof closed, we could “prove” r outright from p → q and q → r — which would be invalid.

Subproofs can be nested. The next proof needs two assumptions, one inside the other.

Worked proof: permuting antecedents

The goal is a conditional with antecedent q, so assume q and aim for p → r; that is itself a conditional, so assume p and aim for r. Line 5 cites line 2, which is accessible because the subproof that begins at line 2 is still open.

The rules for ∨

Disjunction introduction

∨I: from A, write A ∨ B or B ∨ A, for any formula B whatever. (If A is true, “A or anything” is true.)

Disjunction elimination (proof by cases)

Using a disjunction is subtler. If we know A ∨ B, we do not know which disjunct is true. But if C follows from A, and C follows from B, then C follows either way. ∨E cites the disjunction and two subproofs, one for each case, ending with the same formula.

Negation and contradiction

We use the symbol ⊥ to mark a contradiction. There are three basic rules involving it, and one for indirect proof.

Negation elimination

¬E: from A and ¬A, write ⊥. (Some books call this ⊥I.)

Negation introduction

¬I: if assuming A leads to ⊥, close the subproof and write ¬A. This is the classic reductio ad absurdum: a supposition that leads to contradiction must be false.

Explosion

X (ex falso quodlibet): from ⊥, write any formula at all. This matches the semantic fact from Chapter 4 that inconsistent premises entail everything.

Indirect proof

IP: if assuming ¬A leads to ⊥, close the subproof and write A. ¬I lets you refute an assumption; IP lets you prove something by refuting its negation.

IP is the rule that makes the system classical. Intuitionists reject it (Chapter 9): for them, deriving a contradiction from ¬A shows only ¬¬A, not A.

The rules for ↔

↔I: to prove A ↔ B, give two subproofs: one from A to B, one from B to A. ↔E: from A ↔ B and either side, infer the other side.

Strategies for finding proofs

Checking a proof is mechanical; finding one takes practice. These strategies, applied in order, will solve most problems.

  1. Work backwards from the goal, by its main connective.
    • Goal A → B: assume A, aim for B, then →I.
    • Goal A ∧ B: prove A and B separately, then ∧I.
    • Goal ¬A: assume A, aim for ⊥, then ¬I.
    • Goal A ↔ B: two subproofs, then ↔I.
    • Goal A ∨ B: if you can prove one disjunct, use ∨I. Otherwise see strategy 4.
  2. Work forwards from what you have, using elimination rules: split conjunctions with ∧E; apply →E whenever you have a conditional and its antecedent; use ↔E similarly.
  3. If you have a disjunction, consider ∨E: set up the two cases, each aiming for your current goal.
  4. If all else fails, use indirect proof: assume the negation of the goal and aim for ⊥. To get ⊥, look for a formula A such that you can prove both A and ¬A. Often A is a formula already in front of you — a premise or the assumption itself.

Two further tips. Write the goal at the bottom of your page first and work up; a proof is easier to find from both ends. And when you set up a subproof, write its final line straight away, so that you know what you are aiming for.

Strategy in action: exportation

Goal: (p ∧ q) → r ⊢ p → (q → r). The goal is a conditional, so assume p, aiming for q → r. That is a conditional, so assume q, aiming for r. Now work forwards: we have p and q, so p ∧ q, so r by →E.

Strategy in action: Peirce's law

Goal: ⊢ ((p → q) → p) → p. Assume the antecedent, aiming for p. Forward steps are unavailable (we lack p → q), so use IP: assume ¬p. Now we can prove p → q: assuming p contradicts ¬p, and from ⊥ anything follows. Then →E gives p, contradicting ¬p.

Derived rules

Once a pattern has been proved, there is no need to prove it again each time. A derived rule is a rule whose every application could be replaced by a sequence of basic rules. Adding derived rules makes proofs shorter without making anything new provable. We have already proved the patterns behind the following:

Why does a proof of one instance justify a rule for every instance? Because our proofs used only the form of the formulas. Replace p and q throughout by any formulas A and B, and the proof remains correct. In the proof checker you can switch derived rules off to practise with basic rules only.

Affirming the consequent with →E

Applying a rule to part of a line

Rules apply to the main connective of a whole line. Line 1 is a conditional, not a conjunction, so ∧E does not apply. (Indeed p does not follow at all.)

Ending inside a subproof

A proof whose last line is still indented is incomplete: its conclusion depends on an assumption that has not been discharged. Always check that your final line is on the outermost bar.

Truth trees

Natural deduction is one proof method; truth trees (also called semantic tableaux) are another, closely tied to truth tables. A tree tests whether a set of formulas is consistent by systematically trying to make them all true. To test an argument, list the premises and the negation of the conclusion: the argument is valid exactly when this set is inconsistent.

The rules

Each rule breaks a formula into simpler ones. Some rules stack (both results go on the same branch: both must be true); others branch (the branch splits: at least one must be true). Tick a formula when you have applied its rule.

FormulaRuleFormulaRule
A ∧ Bstack A, B¬(A ∧ B)branch ¬A | ¬B
A ∨ Bbranch A | B¬(A ∨ B)stack ¬A, ¬B
A → Bbranch ¬A | B¬(A → B)stack A, ¬B
A ↔ Bbranch (A, B) | (¬A, ¬B)¬(A ↔ B)branch (A, ¬B) | (¬A, B)
¬¬Astack A

A branch closes (marked ×) when it contains some formula and its negation: no row can make everything on it true. When every formula has been ticked (or is a sentence letter or negated letter), a branch that has not closed is open, and it describes a row making all the original formulas true. Read the row off the letters on the branch.

  • If every branch closes, the set is inconsistent — so the argument is valid.
  • If some branch remains open, the set is consistent — the argument is invalid, and the open branch gives a counterexample.

Tip: apply stacking rules before branching rules; it keeps trees small.

Trees and natural deduction always agree on propositional arguments, because both are sound and complete (Chapter 8). Trees are more mechanical — you never need a clever idea — while natural deduction proofs are often shorter and closer to ordinary reasoning.

Summary

  • A proof is a sequence of lines, each a premise, an assumption, or the result of a rule applied to accessible earlier lines.
  • Each connective has an introduction and an elimination rule. →I, ∨E, ¬I, ↔I and IP use subproofs, which begin with an assumption and must be closed.
  • Lines inside a closed subproof cannot be cited; the conclusion must be on the main bar.
  • Strategy: work backwards from the goal's main connective; work forwards with elimination rules; use ∨E on disjunctions; fall back on indirect proof.
  • Derived rules (MT, DS, HS, DNE, DNI, DeM, TND) abbreviate sequences of basic rules.
  • Truth trees test consistency: an argument is valid iff the tree for its premises plus negated conclusion closes.

Key terms

Proof
A finite sequence of formulas, each a premise, assumption or the result of applying a rule to earlier lines.
Natural deduction
A proof system using introduction and elimination rules for each connective, with temporary assumptions.
Fitch-style proof
A natural-deduction proof laid out in numbered lines with vertical bars marking subproofs.
Assumption
In natural deduction, a formula supposed "for the sake of argument" at the head of a subproof; it must be discharged.
Subproof
A part of a natural-deduction proof that begins with an assumption and is later closed (discharged).
Discharge
Closing a subproof so that its assumption no longer counts as a premise of later lines.
Reductio ad absurdum
Proving a claim by showing that its denial leads to a contradiction (or proving ¬A by deriving a contradiction from A).
Explosion
The principle that from a contradiction anything follows (ex falso quodlibet).
Modus ponens
The valid form "If A then B; A; therefore B" (→E).
Modus tollens
The valid form "If A then B; not B; therefore not A".
Disjunctive syllogism
The valid form "A or B; not A; therefore B".
Truth tree
A proof method (semantic tableau) that tests for consistency by systematically searching for a model.
