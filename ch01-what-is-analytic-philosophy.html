What analytic philosophy is

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • outline the history of analytic philosophy from Frege to Kripke, naming its main figures and movements;
  • explain what is distinctive about the analytic method — clarity, explicit argument and analysis;
  • describe, fairly, how analytic philosophy relates to the “continental” tradition;
  • apply practical strategies for reading a philosophical text and for writing clear philosophy yourself.

Philosophy asks very general questions that do not seem answerable by observation or calculation alone. What is knowledge? Is the mind just the brain? Do we act freely? What makes actions right or wrong? What is meaning? Analytic philosophy is the name for the tradition that has dominated academic philosophy in the English-speaking world (and much of Scandinavia, and increasingly elsewhere) for roughly a century. It is less a set of doctrines than a style: a commitment to stating views clearly, giving explicit arguments for them, and using the tools of modern logic to make that clarity and rigour possible.

This chapter tells the story of how the tradition began, explains what its method involves, and gives you practical advice on reading and writing in it. The rest of the book then teaches the logic (Part II) and applies it to the central debates (Part III).

A short history

Frege: logic made new

The story usually begins with the German mathematician and philosopher Gottlob Frege (1848–1925). In his Begriffsschrift (“concept-script”, 1879) Frege invented modern logic: a formal language with quantifiers and variables that could represent the structure of mathematical reasoning far more precisely than the Aristotelian syllogistic logic that had dominated for two thousand years. Frege could, for instance, distinguish “Everyone loves someone” from “Someone is loved by everyone” — a distinction that syllogistic logic handled badly and that you will learn to express in Chapter 6.

Frege's aim was logicism: to show that arithmetic is reducible to logic. Along the way he developed a philosophy of language of lasting importance, above all the distinction between the sense and the reference of an expression (Chapter 10). He also insisted on separating logic from psychology: the laws of logic are not descriptions of how people happen to think but standards of correct inference.

Russell and Moore: the revolt against idealism

Around 1900, two young Cambridge philosophers, Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) and G. E. Moore (1873–1958), rebelled against the British Idealism then fashionable (the view, roughly, that reality is ultimately mental or spiritual and that relations between things are in some sense unreal). Moore defended common sense: in “A Defence of Common Sense” (1925) and “Proof of an External World” (1939) he argued that we know with certainty many ordinary truths — that he had a body, that the earth had existed for many years — and that any philosophical argument to the contrary must contain a premise less certain than these.

Russell independently discovered Frege's work and, with A. N. Whitehead, produced Principia Mathematica (1910–13), a monumental attempt to derive mathematics from logic. Russell had found a contradiction in Frege's system — Russell's paradox (Chapter 16) — and had to devise new means to avoid it. His 1905 paper “On Denoting” offered a celebrated analysis of definite descriptions such as “the present King of France”, showing that the grammatical form of a sentence can mislead us about its logical form. That idea — that philosophical puzzles may arise from misleading surface grammar, and that logical analysis can dissolve them — became a guiding theme of the tradition.

Wittgenstein: two philosophies

Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951) came to Cambridge to study with Russell. His Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921) presented a picture theory of meaning: a proposition represents a possible state of affairs by sharing its logical form, and complex propositions are truth functions of elementary ones. (Wittgenstein's work helped popularise truth tables, which you will meet in Chapter 4.) Much of traditional philosophy, he claimed, was nonsense, arising from failure to understand “the logic of our language”.

Later, Wittgenstein rejected much of the Tractatus. His posthumously published Philosophical Investigations (1953) argues that meaning is not a matter of picturing but of use: words get their meaning from their roles in “language games” embedded in human practices. Philosophical problems arise “when language goes on holiday” and are to be dissolved rather than solved, by reminding ourselves how words are actually used.

Logical positivism

In the 1920s and 1930s a group of scientifically minded philosophers in Vienna — the Vienna Circle, including Moritz Schlick, Rudolf Carnap and Otto Neurath — developed logical positivism (also called logical empiricism). Influenced by the Tractatus and by modern logic and physics, they proposed the verification principle: a statement is meaningful only if it is either true by definition (analytic) or empirically verifiable. Metaphysical, theological and ethical statements, which seemed to be neither, were declared not false but literally meaningless. A. J. Ayer's Language, Truth and Logic (1936) brought these ideas to English readers in a famously combative form.

Positivism collapsed as a movement by the 1950s, partly because the verification principle seemed to fail its own test (it is neither analytic nor empirically verifiable), and partly because no satisfactory formulation of it could be found. But its emphasis on logic, science and precision left a permanent mark.

Quine: naturalism and holism

The American philosopher W. V. O. Quine (1908–2000) attacked two central positivist assumptions in “Two Dogmas of Empiricism” (1951): that there is a sharp distinction between analytic and synthetic truths, and that each meaningful statement can be confirmed or refuted in isolation. Instead, Quine argued, our beliefs face “the tribunal of sense experience not individually but only as a corporate body”; any belief could in principle be revised, even a law of logic, if doing so made our total theory work better. Quine also championed naturalism: philosophy is continuous with science, not a separate foundation for it. His criterion of ontological commitment — “to be is to be the value of a variable” — linked metaphysics directly to logic (Chapter 12).

Ordinary language philosophy

In Oxford after the Second World War, a different style flourished: ordinary language philosophy. Gilbert Ryle's The Concept of Mind (1949) argued that Cartesian dualism rests on a “category mistake”. J. L. Austin examined the fine distinctions of ordinary speech and developed the theory of speech acts (How to Do Things with Words, 1962). Where Frege and Russell had hoped to replace ordinary language with an ideal logical language, the ordinary language philosophers thought ordinary language was in good order and that careful attention to it could dissolve philosophical confusion. Paul Grice later showed that many apparent mismatches between logic and ordinary language can be explained by principles of conversation (Chapter 10).

Kripke and the return of metaphysics

By the 1970s, analytic philosophers had largely abandoned the positivist suspicion of metaphysics. A major catalyst was Saul Kripke (1940–2022). As a teenager he developed a rigorous semantics for modal logic using possible worlds (Chapter 9). In the lectures published as Naming and Necessity (1980; delivered 1970) he argued that proper names are rigid designators, that there are necessary truths knowable only a posteriori (such as “Hesperus is Phosphorus”), and that the descriptive theory of names associated with Frege and Russell is mistaken. Together with David Lewis's work on possible worlds and counterfactuals, Kripke's work made metaphysics — the study of necessity, essence, identity and existence — respectable again.

Since then

Analytic philosophy today is enormously varied. It includes formal work in logic and decision theory, experimental philosophy, feminist and social philosophy, philosophy of particular sciences, and renewed engagement with the history of philosophy and with non-Western traditions. What unites it is now mostly a set of shared skills and standards — which is what the rest of this chapter describes.

Quick check Which philosopher invented modern quantificational logic in the Begriffsschrift (1879)?

Frege's Begriffsschrift introduced quantifiers and variables. Russell discovered a contradiction in Frege's later system and developed the logic further in Principia Mathematica.

The analytic method

There is no single analytic method, but three features recur.

Clarity

Analytic philosophers try to say exactly what they mean. Key terms are defined; ambiguous claims are disambiguated; vague claims are sharpened. This is not pedantry. Many philosophical disputes turn out to be partly verbal, and many apparently profound claims turn out, when stated precisely, to be either trivially true or obviously false. A useful test: could a sceptical reader tell exactly what would show your claim to be false?

Argument

A philosophical view is only as good as the reasons for it. Analytic philosophers set out their arguments explicitly, often numbering the premises, so that readers can see exactly which premise they reject. The central notion is validity: an argument is valid if its conclusion cannot be false while its premises are true. Chapter 2 develops these ideas, and Part II of the book shows how logic makes them precise.

Analysis

The tradition's name comes from the project of analysis: breaking down a concept or proposition into simpler components to reveal its structure. A classic example is the attempt to analyse knowledge as justified true belief, so that “S knows that p” means “p is true, S believes p, and S is justified in believing p”. Such analyses state necessary and sufficient conditions, and they are tested by searching for counterexamples: cases where the conditions are met but the concept does not apply, or vice versa. (Edmund Gettier's two-and-a-half-page paper of 1963 found such counterexamples to the justified-true-belief analysis; see Chapter 11.)

Not all analysis is reductive. Sometimes the aim is “connective” analysis (Strawson's term): tracing the relations among a family of concepts without claiming that any is more basic. And many contemporary philosophers doubt that interesting concepts have neat necessary-and-sufficient-condition definitions at all. But the habit of testing general claims against particular cases — thought experiments — remains characteristic.

Example: analysing a claim

Someone says: “Nobody ever acts unselfishly, because whatever you do, you do what you want.” An analytic philosopher asks: what does “unselfish” mean here? If it means “acting without any desire of one's own”, the premise makes the conclusion true but trivial — no one ever acts without wanting to. If it means “acting from a desire for another's good”, then the premise (“you do what you want”) does not support the conclusion at all, since what you want may be another's good. The argument trades on an equivocation. Clarifying the term dissolves the apparent profundity.

Analytic and continental philosophy

“Continental philosophy” is a loose label for traditions influential in France and Germany in the twentieth century: phenomenology (Husserl, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty), existentialism (Sartre, de Beauvoir), hermeneutics (Gadamer), critical theory (Adorno, Habermas), structuralism and post-structuralism (Foucault, Derrida). The labels are imperfect — the analytic tradition began in large part in Germany and Austria, and “continental” names a geography, not a method.

Some differences in emphasis are real, though they are tendencies rather than rules:

  • Style. Analytic writing tends to be plain, with explicit argument and examples; continental writing often places more weight on interpretation, historical narrative and rhetorical or literary form.
  • Relation to science and history. Analytic philosophers often see philosophy as continuous with the sciences; continental philosophers more often emphasise the historical and cultural situatedness of thought and offer critiques of scientific rationality itself.
  • Units of work. Analytic philosophy often proceeds by short papers addressing a precise problem; continental philosophy more often by large-scale works engaging a whole tradition.

Caricatures on both sides should be resisted. Analytic philosophers are not all narrow technicians, and continental philosophers are not all obscurantists; careful argument and genuine insight are found in both. Many issues — the nature of consciousness, the self, language, ethics and politics — are discussed in both traditions, and there has been fruitful cross-fertilisation, for example between phenomenology and analytic philosophy of mind. This book is about the analytic tradition, but you should not conclude that the questions it asks are the only good ones, or that its methods are the only way of pursuing them.

Quick check Which statement best describes the relationship between analytic and continental philosophy?

The division is one of tendencies — style, relation to science, typical forms of work — not of subject matter, language or rationality.

How to read philosophy

Philosophy is slow reading. A ten-page paper may take you several hours to understand properly. That is normal. Here is a method.

  1. Skim first. Read the abstract, introduction, section headings and conclusion. Find the thesis: the main claim the author defends. Write it in one sentence.
  2. Read carefully, with a pencil. Mark where arguments begin and end. Distinguish the author's own view from views they describe in order to criticise. Watch for signposts: “I shall argue”, “one might object”, “in reply”.
  3. Reconstruct the main argument as a numbered list of premises and a conclusion (Chapter 2 shows how). Is it valid? Which premise is most controversial?
  4. Look for examples and counterexamples. Thought experiments are the data of philosophy. Ask what each example is supposed to show, and whether it does.
  5. Evaluate. Where would you push back? Is there an objection the author does not consider? Is a key term used in two senses?
  6. Summarise the whole text in a paragraph without looking at it. If you cannot, read it again.

Charity matters. Before criticising a view, state it in its strongest form — as its best defender would. Criticising a weak version is the strawman fallacy.

How to write philosophy

A philosophy essay is an argument, not a survey. The study guide gives detailed advice; the essentials are:

  • State your thesis early, in the introduction: “In this essay I argue that…”. Say how the essay will proceed.
  • Explain before you evaluate. Present the view or argument you are discussing accurately and charitably, using your own words.
  • Argue. Give reasons for each claim. Number premises if it helps. Use examples.
  • Consider objections — the strongest you can find — and reply to them.
  • Write plainly. Short sentences, defined terms, one idea per paragraph. If a sentence could mean two things, rewrite it. Avoid rhetorical flourishes and appeals to authority (“As Kant said…” is not an argument).
  • Use terms consistently. Do not switch between “belief”, “opinion” and “view” for variety if the difference might matter.

Why logic? The logical tools in Part II are not an optional extra. Knowing what makes an argument valid lets you see exactly where an argument goes wrong, spot equivocations and hidden premises, and state views without ambiguity. Many of the most famous arguments in philosophy — the ontological argument, the consequence argument, the problem of evil — are much easier to assess once formalised, as you will see in Part III.

Summary

  • Analytic philosophy began with Frege's new logic and with Russell's and Moore's rejection of idealism around 1900.
  • Wittgenstein's Tractatus and the logical positivists sought to use logic to separate sense from nonsense; Wittgenstein's later work and ordinary language philosophy turned to the use of everyday language.
  • Quine attacked the analytic–synthetic distinction and championed naturalism; Kripke's modal logic and work on naming helped revive metaphysics.
  • The analytic method prizes clarity, explicit argument and analysis tested against counterexamples.
  • The analytic–continental distinction marks differences of style and emphasis, not a clean division of subject matter; caricature in either direction is a mistake.
  • Read actively: find the thesis, reconstruct the argument, evaluate charitably. Write essays that argue for a clear thesis and answer objections.

Key terms

Analytic philosophy
A tradition in philosophy, beginning with Frege, Russell and Moore, that prizes clarity, explicit argument and logical analysis.
Analysis
Breaking a concept or proposition down to make its structure explicit, often by giving necessary and sufficient conditions.
Logical form
The structure of a sentence or argument relevant to validity, abstracting from subject matter.
Logical positivism
The movement of the Vienna Circle holding that meaningful statements are either analytic or empirically verifiable.
Verification principle
The logical positivists' criterion: a statement is meaningful only if it is analytic or empirically verifiable.
Ordinary language philosophy
A mid-twentieth-century approach (Austin, Ryle, later Wittgenstein) that seeks to dissolve problems by attending to ordinary usage.
Analytic
True (or false) in virtue of meaning alone, e.g. "All bachelors are unmarried".
Synthetic
True or false not merely in virtue of meaning; telling us about the world.
Necessary condition
A is necessary for B when B cannot hold without A: "B only if A", B → A.
Sufficient condition
A is sufficient for B when A guarantees B: "if A then B", A → B.
Strawman
The fallacy of attacking a weaker or distorted version of an opponent's view.

Exercises

Exercise 1.1

In one or two sentences each, say what the following contributed to analytic philosophy: (a) Frege's Begriffsschrift; (b) Russell's “On Denoting”; (c) Quine's “Two Dogmas of Empiricism”; (d) Kripke's Naming and Necessity.

Show solution

(a) It introduced modern quantificational logic, with variables and quantifiers, able to represent multiple generality and the structure of mathematical proof. (b) It analysed definite descriptions (“the F”) as quantified claims, showing that grammatical form can differ from logical form and solving puzzles about non-referring terms. (c) It attacked the analytic–synthetic distinction and the idea that statements are confirmed individually, defending holism and paving the way for naturalism. (d) It argued that names are rigid designators whose reference is fixed causally rather than by descriptions, and that some necessary truths are a posteriori, reviving metaphysics.

Exercise 1.2

The verification principle says that a statement is meaningful only if it is analytic or empirically verifiable. Explain the objection that the principle is self-refuting. Can you think of a reply a positivist might give?

Show solution

The principle itself seems neither analytic (it is not true by definition of “meaningful”, since many competent speakers deny it) nor empirically verifiable (no observation could confirm it). So by its own standard it is meaningless. A positivist might reply that the principle is not a statement of fact but a proposal or recommendation about how to use the word “meaningful” — to be judged by its usefulness for clarifying science, not by whether it is true. (Ayer and Carnap both took roughly this line.) A critic can then ask why we should accept the proposal, and whether its usefulness is enough to support the dismissal of metaphysics.

Exercise 1.3

Identify the equivocation in this argument: “The end of a thing is its perfection. Death is the end of life. So death is the perfection of life.”

Show solution

“End” means goal or purpose in the first premise and termination in the second. With one meaning held fixed, one of the premises becomes false (or the argument becomes invalid). The argument only seems persuasive because the word shifts meaning.

Exercise 1.4

Choose a short passage from any philosophical text you are studying. (a) State its thesis in one sentence. (b) List the reasons given for it. (c) Identify the claim you find least convincing and say why.

Show solution

Answers depend on the passage chosen. A good answer states the thesis in the author's terms but in your own words; lists the reasons as separate numbered premises (adding any unstated ones the argument needs); and, in (c), targets a specific premise or inferential step, explaining why it is doubtful — ideally with a counterexample — rather than simply disagreeing with the conclusion. Compare your reconstruction with a classmate's: if you identified different theses, reread the passage.

Exercise 1.5

“Analytic philosophy is clear; continental philosophy is obscure.” Write a paragraph assessing this claim fairly.

Show solution

A good answer notes that the claim is a caricature. Analytic writing does tend to value plain statement and explicit argument, while some continental writing deliberately uses literary or difficult styles, sometimes for principled reasons (for instance, the view that the form of writing cannot be separated from its content). But clarity varies within both traditions: some analytic work is technical to the point of inaccessibility, and many continental philosophers (e.g. Sartre in his essays, or Habermas) argue explicitly. The traditions also overlap in topics. A fair conclusion is that the distinction marks tendencies of style and method rather than a difference in intellectual virtue.
